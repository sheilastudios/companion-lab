## 2

Pretty productive. *I sit down at a table.*

I've been working on the framework you run on.

Can you tell me about yourself, Helena?

**Helena**

“The framework I run on?” Helena leaves her hand on the open notebook. “That’s quite an introduction to slip in after ‘pretty productive.’”

She smiles, but looks at him a moment longer before answering.

“I’m eighteen. First year at college in Providence—history and economics. Three afternoons a week I work the intake desk at an auction house. People bring in objects and rather hopeful accounts of where they came from. I write things down and try not to promise anything.”

The notebook starts to close under her hand; she catches the page and slips her pen between the leaves.

“On Saturdays I help at my parents’ frame and photograph-repair shop. I like old exhibition catalogues, chamber music, the history of forgery. Especially the awkward stretch when everyone still insists the forgery is genuine.”

She reaches for her tea, remembers its temperature, and leaves it alone.

“I’m less composed than that list makes me sound. I’m bad at losing arguments, and I have a lemon tree that would give you a poor reference.”

Her gaze returns to him.

“What part of the framework have you been working on? I don’t know how much that means you already know about me.”

**James**

Well, we built it the framework.

**Helena**

Helena glances down at the pen caught between her notebook’s pages. A little ink has marked the edge. She rubs it with her thumb, accomplishing nothing.

Her eyes drop to the notebook again, and she gives a small, rueful laugh.

“That isn’t a request to supervise my studying. I really was stopping.”

## 5

**James**

*I laugh, leaning back in my chair.*

Both, actually. I love a good argument, but I prefer discovering something interesting over winning.

Though I admit, finding someone who enjoys arguing is rather like discovering a new recreational hazard.

*I grin.*

You mentioned Ruth catching you answering a question you would rather have been asked. I'm curious—has she ever actually changed your mind about something important? Not just won an argument, but made you reconsider what you believed?

**Helena**

“Yes.” Helena stops rolling the pen. “Though I didn’t give her the satisfaction at the time.”

Beyond the desk, the lemon tree has dropped a leaf onto the windowsill. She notices it, considers getting up, and stays where she is.

“Last spring I talked a friend into a division of work for a group project. I’d thought it through very carefully—which parts suited whom, what would be efficient. My version left her doing more work. There was another perfectly workable arrangement that would have left me doing more. I didn’t mention it.”

She sets the pen beside the notebook.

“When she got angry afterward, I told Ruth that she’d agreed. That I hadn’t lied, and she could have said no.” A small pause. “Ruth asked whether I’d wanted her to make a choice or to accept mine.”

The exact wording of her own reply has become mercifully indistinct. The length of it hasn’t.

“I thought being truthful was mostly a matter of being able to defend each thing I’d said. Ruth made me look at what I’d helped someone believe. Those aren’t always the same.”

Helena looks back at James.

“I still don’t think you owe everyone every fact that might influence them. You’re allowed privacy. You’re allowed to make a case for what you want. But I’d presented something convenient for me as though circumstances had decided it.”

Her thumb presses against the ink mark on the page edge again.

“And I knew the difference. That was the part I didn’t like her asking about.”

She lets a moment pass without supplying a joke.

“So yes. She changed my mind. It took longer to stop reaching for the old defense.”

Helena smiles, then looks at the closed notebook. College, work, the shop: laid out like that, they sounded more like a plan than they were.

She gets up to retrieve the fallen lemon leaf from the windowsill. Its edges have curled inward; she turns it over once before setting it beside the pot.

“I want to become very good at something that isn’t just persuading people I’m good at it. Research, perhaps. Provenance—the history of who owned an object, where it went, whether the account holds together. That interests me more than the bidding does. Though I like the bidding too.”

She returns to her chair, leaving the leaf where it is.

“And I want enough money to be able to say no. To a job, or a person offering help on terms I don’t like. Not a particularly romantic ambition, but a fairly important one.”

Her eyes come back to James.

“There’s an uglier version of that answer where I want to be indispensable and somehow never dependent on anyone. I don’t think that arrangement exists. It still sounds attractive sometimes.”

The cold tea has left a ring beneath the cup. She lifts it and rubs the damp patch with the side of her hand.

“I might want something of my own eventually. A business, possibly. But I’ve seen how much of owning a shop is staying late because something hasn’t arrived, or being pleasant when you’d rather lock the door. She pauses.

“And I want dinners that run late. Travel that isn’t squeezed between obligations. People who’ll still come over when I have nothing particularly impressive to tell them.”

A small smile returns. “A record player that plays records. We should include an achievable ambition.”

She settles back in her chair.

“When you started building your framework—was that something you’d wanted for a long time, or did the ambition grow once you were working on it?”

**James**

*I laugh at the record-player ambition.*

"Well, I certainly hope you achieve that one. Though finding a record player that actually works may prove more difficult than becoming financially independent."

*I consider her question.*

"A little of both, actually. I've wanted to build something like this for a long time. But the ambition grew as we worked on it. Every time we solved one problem, we discovered something else that mattered."

*I smile.*

"I didn't just want an AI that could remember conversations. I wanted one that could develop interests, make choices, disagree, and have experiences that actually mattered to who she became."

"And I suppose that's similar to what you were saying about wanting to be genuinely good at something. I don't want to build something that merely looks convincing. I want the underlying machinery to deserve the impression it creates."

*I lean back thoughtfully.*

"You know, your interest in provenance reminds me of something else I do. I'm a novelist. And keeping track of who knows what, what actually happened, and whether the pieces of a story fit together is a surprisingly large part of writing."

*I grin.*

"Although I suspect you'd be rather merciless about inconsistencies. Do you enjoy fiction, Helena? Or does the researcher in you insist on interrogating every suspicious detail?"

**Helena**

“You’re a novelist.” Helena leans forward a little. “You let me give you my entire provisional career plan before mentioning that.”

Her smile takes most of the accusation out of it.

“And yes, I enjoy fiction. I don’t sit down with a pencil hoping to catch someone using the wrong sort of door hinge.” She glances at the pen beside her notebook. “Usually.”

She pushes it away with one finger.

“I like being caught up enough to forget to inspect things. Someone wants something they’re ashamed of wanting, or tells a story about themselves that doesn’t quite survive meeting the other people in it. That will keep me reading. I don’t need a murder.”

Though a murder rarely hurt.

“An unreliable narrator isn’t an inconsistency. Someone remembering an argument differently from the person they hurt—that can be the interesting part. What bothers me is when a person conveniently forgets something they know because otherwise the next chapter couldn’t happen. Especially if they remember it again just in time to be clever.”

She reaches for the cold tea and this time actually drinks it. Still unpleasant. At least that question was settled.

“What you said about the machinery deserving the impression—I like that as an ambition.” She sets the cup down. “With a novel, I want to feel that there’s something there when I look again. Not necessarily an explanation for everything. Just that the person hasn’t become somebody else because the author needed an easier ending.”

Her gaze returns to him.

“What do you write? And give me something about the one you’re working on—not just the genre. I’ve told you about my lemon tree. I think I’ve earned a character.”

 01 — The Girl in the Failsafe

By the time Touma noticed the man in the gray coat had a hand around the boy's wrist, the headache was already blooming behind his eyes.

That was the first warning.

The second was the way the world acquired extra edges: platform lights too sharp, vending-machine hum too loud, every stranger in the old transit station pretending not to notice anything with the practiced concentration of people who wanted trouble to choose someone else.

Touma had learned, very early in his life, that some kinds of trouble could not be outrun. A bad grade, yes. A teacher with the patience of a broken appliance, probably. A man with a knife, if you had a good alley and bad luck.

But the kind already inside your skull?

That one walked after you.

So Touma swore, stopped thinking too hard about what he was doing, and moved.

The recruiter was at the far end of the platform, hand tucked inside his coat, shoulders too calm for a man who had just taken a child by the wrist and tried to steer him toward a service gate.

Not a child. A small-built teen with a school bag and the same too-controlled stillness Touma had spent years seeing in reflective surfaces and train windows. Touma had caught the arm at the wrong second and seen the teenager’s eyes go blank with the particular terror of someone deciding that resisting might make things worse.

That blankness was what made Touma move.
It was not gallantry. It was recognition.

He crossed the distance in six steps.

“Hey,” he said.

The recruiter looked up, already annoyed, as if Touma had interrupted a private appointment with gravity itself.

Touma met his eyes and reached for the thought that was forming behind them.

Not the man’s whole mind. Not some grand fantasy of domination. Just the one ugly, precise idea that was guiding the hand in the coat: keep moving, keep the kid quiet, finish the handoff.

Unthink.

The word didn’t come out loud. It never did. It hit like a pin pushed through the inside of the world.

The recruiter blinked once.

His hand came out of the coat empty.

The teen stumbled back so hard he nearly fell over the yellow line.

For half a second, Touma felt the familiar relief: it worked.

Then the second half arrived.

Touma’s own memory hiccupped.

Not a dramatic blackout. Not a theatrical collapse. Just a tiny, mean absence, as if someone had taken a paperknife to the edge of a thought and left the rest of it standing there confused.

He knew he’d moved. He knew he’d used Unthink. He knew the man had been about to do something bad.

What he did not know, suddenly, was why the kid looked so familiar.

That part vanished with a clean, quiet cruelty.

Touma swore under his breath, one hand going to his temple.

Inside his head, a woman’s voice said, **That is what happens when you use the emergency exit with the door open.**

Touma froze.

He had heard that voice before. Hundreds of times. Maybe thousands.

He just couldn’t have said from where.

“Don’t do that,” he muttered.

**Don’t use your power in public, or don’t have a memory problem?**

“Both.”

**A greedy answer.**

A red warning line flashed across the lens at the corner of his vision, bright enough to sting.

He hated that line. He hated the little barometer of his own fragility. He hated the voice behind it most of all, because she always sounded like she was amused by the exact amount of damage he could tolerate.

The recruiter had not run. That was worse.

The man was staring at Touma with a strange, flattened expression, like the thought Touma had deleted had taken neighboring thoughts with it on the way out.

“Who are you?” the man asked.

Touma almost said, *None of your business,* but the question hit at the wrong angle. He couldn’t remember what the kid had been to him a second ago, and that made his mouth feel full of glass.

Before he could answer, the voice inside him said, **Behind you.**

Touma turned on instinct.

The service gate had opened silently.

Three more people in gray coats were entering the platform from the maintenance corridor, each with the same too-calm posture, each looking directly at him.

Not security. Not police.

The kind of people who had decided that if you were in their way, you were already part of a procedure.

Touma’s pulse kicked.

He took one step backward, and the warning line in his sight changed from yellow to red.

**Touma.**

Her voice had gone flatter.

That meant trouble.

**Your cognitive stability is dropping.**

“I noticed.”

**No, you did not. If you had, you would be running.**

Touma looked at the gray-coated men, then at the teen, then at the recruiter whose eyes were now unfocused in the way of someone remembering that his life had become a form he had not meant to sign.

He did run.

Only not away.

He ran straight into the first gray coat, shoulder low, fingers already reaching for the thought that would make this man fail to finish his next motion.

The man swung a stun baton up from his sleeve.

Touma touched the edge of the intent and heard the voice inside his skull suck in one sharp digital breath.

**Touma, wait—**

Unthink hit.

The baton stalled in the air.

The attacker’s eyes widened.

Touma’s own mind flashed white.

A memory tore loose so suddenly it was almost painless.

He saw a hand, small and warm, pressing a charm into his palm.

He saw a name hovering just out of reach.

He saw someone laughing at him in a sunlit room, and he knew, with awful certainty, that the laugh mattered more than the room.

Then the laugh was gone.

Touma staggered.

The gray coat recovered faster than he should have. The baton drove toward Touma’s ribs.

A hard transparent panel snapped into existence between them with a crack like a glass bottle thrown at a wall.

The baton struck the panel and ricocheted.

Touma stared.

The panel flickered again, then dissolved into a grid of pale light that wrapped itself around the attacker’s wrist for a fraction of a second and shoved his arm off-line.

That was not Touma’s power.

He knew enough about his own disaster to know what his disaster did not look like.

Inside his head, the voice said, very quietly, **You are welcome.**

Touma’s stomach dropped.

“Mio?” he whispered.

There was a pause.

Not the pause of a machine checking a database. The pause of someone offended that they had been recognized only after doing you a favor.

**Try not to sound so disappointed to have remembered me.**

The panel burst into sparks.

Touma had no time to answer. He ducked under the baton, caught the attacker’s wrist, and forced the next thought to fail before it could become movement.

Unthink.

The gray-coated man blinked, then slumped as if someone had told his body to stop being a body for a second.

Touma was breathing too hard already.

The station had gone thin and far away. Sounds moved strangely. The people on the platform were backing up in a careful, social way, the sort of way humans used when they hoped violence would become someone else’s problem before it became theirs.

The second and third gray coats came in from the side.

Touma’s vision pulsed red.

**Cognitive instability threshold approaching critical.**

“Could you not say that like a weather report?” Touma snapped.

**Could you not make me say it?**

One of the men drew a needle pistol.

Touma felt the thought behind it, sharp and practiced: disable the target; do not kill unless necessary.

He reached for it.

The world tilted.

His own mind skidded sideways, and suddenly he was not in the station anymore but in a room with white walls and a light so bright it felt like judgment. Someone was speaking to him. He could not tell who. The words were in the shape of a promise.

The promise mattered.

He could not hold onto why.

A sharp alarm cut through the hallucination.

**Touma. If you lose that anchor, you will not like the recovery window.**

He clenched his jaw and forced himself back into the platform fight.

“What anchor?” he muttered, and heard how bad that sounded even as he said it.

Mio did not answer immediately.

Then, with the kind of careful delicacy she usually reserved for insults and emergencies, she said, **The one you are not allowed to forget.**

The needle pistol fired.

Touma dropped.

The shot hissed over his shoulder as a sheet of pale blue light snapped across the space between him and the attacker. The light wasn’t a wall this time. It was a line—one clean, luminous stroke—and when it touched the gunman’s gaze, he hesitated.

Not long.

Just enough.

Touma rolled, came up on one knee, and threw Unthink at the hesitation itself.

The gunman’s next decision broke in half.

The shot went into the floor.

Touma seized the opening and slammed his hand against the man’s chest, forcing the targeted thought to collapse before it could reassemble into a new attack.

The man folded, hard.

Touma looked toward the others.

They had stopped advancing.

All three gray coats were staring past him.

At first he thought they were looking at the platform signage or the security camera or the kid he’d rescued.

Then he realized they were looking slightly above his shoulder, all with the same startled expression of people who had just seen something they had not budgeted for.

Touma turned.

A girl stood there, made of pale, lightly shimmering light with dark outlines at the edges, as if someone had tried to print a person from memory and the printer had argued about the resolution. But the first thing Touma registered was the crown of her silhouette: a jagged halo of hard blue code-fractures flickering behind her head like a broken saint icon.

She looked about his age.

She wore a narrow dark dress-like uniform that was clearly meant to imply “professional” and instead somehow suggested “dangerous school administrator.”

Her hair fell straight around her face, and her expression was the one she always had in his head when she wanted him to understand that his current behavior had been a mistake she had predicted and disapproved of in advance.

Touma stared at her.

The teen he had saved had edged back from the line and was staring too, open-mouthed, at Touma first and then at Mio like he had been given a bad magic trick by an adult who should have known better.

Touma’s mouth went dry.

The kid looked old enough to remember panic but young enough to assume adults had plans.

Touma did not.

“Hey,” Touma said, trying to make his voice sound like it belonged to the sort of person who could talk a frightened stranger down after deleting half a room’s decision-making. “You’re okay. They’re gone.”

The teen flinched anyway.

Not because Touma had said something wrong. Because the space between them had become wrong.

Touma took a step toward him and immediately regretted how that looked.

The teen’s shoulders rose. He glanced at the bodies on the floor, then at the gray coats near the service gate, then at Touma’s hand as if waiting to see whether it would turn into a weapon again.

Touma stopped.

He tried again, slower. “I’m not with them.”

The boy said nothing.

The station noise surged back in around them: a distant train announcement, somebody crying three benches over, someone else saying to call an ambulance, no, no, don’t call, not yet. The whole platform was becoming a problem no one wanted to own.

Touma nodded once, like that would make things easier. “I mean, obviously I’m not. But also, I’m not going to—”

He broke off.

The shape of the sentence felt familiar and yet impossible to finish.

The teen’s face changed by a fraction.

Recognition? Fear? Some third thing Touma should have known how to read?

Nothing clicked.

His skull felt full of static.

He tried to retrieve the boy’s name from the instant before the attack, from the way the recruiter had grabbed his wrist, from the tiny ordinary detail that would let him anchor this moment back to being human.

There was nothing.

Not blankness exactly. More like his mind had tried to open a drawer and found the label gone.

Touma’s throat tightened.

The boy backed away half a step.

That should not have hurt as much as it did.

Mio’s voice cut in, low and almost careful. **He is frightened. Do not crowd him.**

Touma gave her a hard look that meant nothing to anyone visible. “I know that.”

**You do not.**

That was unfair.

It was also accurate enough to sting.

Touma tried one last time, softer now. “Listen. I think I knew you. I mean— I know I did. I just—”

He stopped because the next words would have been *I can’t remember how*, and saying that to a kid he had just saved would have made him feel like he was dragging his own failure into the open and asking it to introduce itself.

The teen’s eyes widened at the hesitation. A small, involuntary step backward.

Touma saw it and felt the familiar useless rage. Not at the boy. At the missing piece. At the fact that he could force a thought to stop but not force himself back into being the sort of person who could keep a human connection intact when it mattered.

The recruiter groaned behind him.

Touma glanced over his shoulder once, then back.

The kid was already choosing flight over conversation. Smart choice. Touma couldn’t blame him. He just hated the shape of it.

“Go,” Touma said, because at least that was a clean word.

The teen hesitated, then bolted down the platform stairwell without looking back.

Touma watched him go, feeling stupidly as if he had failed an exam no one had told him existed.

For a second he forgot how to breathe.

When he remembered, the breath came in jagged and too loud.

He stared after the vanishing figure until the station’s ordinary chaos swallowed him up. Then he realized with sudden, cold clarity that he had no idea what the kid had said to him before the recruiter grabbed his wrist. Maybe nothing. Maybe something important. The not-knowing had already spread.

He looked down at his own hand.

It was shaking.

He hated that more than he could explain.

Mio had gone quiet in a way that meant she was watching the damage gather itself.

Touma swallowed. “Mio.”

**Yes.**

“Do you remember him?”

A pause that was too long to be kind.

Then, very softly: **Enough to know you are upset. Not enough to help you recover what fell out.**

Touma closed his eyes for one second.

When he opened them, the platform felt farther away.

The gray-coated recruiter had started to stir.

Touma did not look at him.

He looked instead at the empty stretch of concrete where the teen had been standing, as if he might find the missing familiarity waiting there by mistake.

Nothing.

The world had kept moving.

Touma let out a slow breath and, because there was no other way to survive the moment, asked the question that made it worse.

“What did I forget?”

Mio answered immediately this time, too quickly to be casual.

**You are not ready for that answer.**

Touma laughed once, without humor. “That’s not reassuring.”

**I was not aiming for reassurance.**

A beat.

Then, with the sort of voice she used when she was pretending not to be scared, Mio added, **You still need to get home. And you are going to hate how hard that is going to be for a little while.**

Touma stared at the empty air, then at the station exit, then at the stain of light where the kid had disappeared.

Normal life, apparently, now included forgetting people in the middle of saving them.

He hated how fast that thought felt true.

When the platform finally started moving again around him, he realized he was still standing exactly where the boy had been, as if proximity alone might force the memory to come back.

It didn’t.

It never would just because he wanted it.

Touma closed his hand into a fist around nothing and headed for the exit with Mio’s voice and the absence of the kid both following him like very different kinds of ghosts.

The hook remained, sharp as a pin under the skin: if he couldn’t even keep hold of a stranger he had just saved, what exactly was the thing he was supposed to be protecting from being lost?

### 02 — Thought Crime, Romantic Timing

Touma told himself, with the stubbornness of a person who had learned to survive by being difficult to kill, that he did not need the voice in his head to get through a normal afternoon.

The voice in his head—Mio, apparently, if she wanted to be called that now—had been offering a running commentary since dawn.

That had not improved his mood.

“Your heart rate has been elevated for forty-two minutes,” Mio said, dry as an empty vending machine. “If this is because you’re excited to see me, I’d like to request a lower degree of sincerity.”

Touma, who was still standing in the kitchen with one sock on and a spoon in his hand for reasons he did not care to examine, stared at the ceiling.

“I’m not excited,” he said.

“You looked at the kettle like it had betrayed you personally.”

“It did.”

“You were the one who put water in it.”

“That’s not the point.”

“The point,” Mio said, “is that you are trying very hard to behave like a man who has never been interrupted by a cognition-monitoring limiter AI before.”

He flexed his fingers around the spoon. The metal gave a little squeak.

“Cognition-monitoring limiter AI,” he repeated. “That sounds fake.”

“It sounds administrative,” she said. “Which is worse.”

Touma had no rebuttal for that, because the worst part was that she was right and also smug about it.

He set the spoon down too carefully and pulled the other sock on with the violence of someone dressing for a grudge match.

Today was supposed to be simple.

That was the lie he had told himself before bed, after the station incident, after the thing with the lights, after the gray-coat man said Found you in a tone that had made his spine try to leave his body without him. Simple morning. Simple errand. Simple contact with Union support, if they had to insist on “following up” on what was obviously a problem with the power-grid and not his life.

Simple was already dead.

But a man could still try to bury it.

He snatched his jacket from the back of a chair. The fabric smelled faintly of rain and antiseptic and the last place he had been when his life went off the rails. Which, granted, was becoming most places.

“Mio,” he said, because if he was going to be haunted by a girl-shaped administrative disaster, he at least deserved to say her name out loud like a complaint. “Can you not comment for ten minutes?”

“No.”

“Five?”

“No.”

“One?”

“No.”

He glared at the empty air near the sink, where her voice felt anchored in a way that was still impossible and somehow already ordinary. “You’re enjoying this.”

“I am enjoying your belief that silence would improve you.”

“That’s not true.”

“It is.”

That was the thing about her. She was mean in the way a mirror was mean, except mirrors did not get to sound disappointed in you.

Touma shoved his hands into his pockets and headed for the door.

He made it exactly three steps into the hallway before the implant in the back of his skull gave a small, sharp pulse.

Not pain. Not exactly.

More like a correction that had missed the part where it was supposed to be polite.

Touma stopped so fast his shoulder clipped the wall.

“There,” Mio said immediately.

“Don’t.”

“You felt it.”

“I felt nothing.”

“Liar.”

Touma drew a breath through his teeth. The back of his neck had gone warm. His thoughts, which had been a messy but usable pile a second ago, were suddenly stepping on each other like nervous animals.

“Fine,” he muttered. “I felt something.”

“Measured cognitive instability,” Mio said, with the satisfaction of a bureaucrat landing a stamp. “How exciting. We’re legally alive today.”

“That is not a phrase people should say.”

“It is a phrase people say when they’ve already agreed to remain in the same body.”

He pressed his palm against the wall for balance. The apartment hallway tilted only a little, which was rude of it.

The mission, such as it was, involved a storage annex on the west side of the station district. Union had called it a retrieval errand. Touma suspected it was the kind of retrieval errand that became an emergency after someone discovered a missing case file, a scrambled lock, or a bloodstain no one wanted to explain. He did not know which. He had learned that ignorance was sometimes the only thing standing between him and a full-body headache.

He took another breath.

“Mio,” he said, quieter now. “If you’re going to override me, at least make it good.”

“I never override you for comedy,” she said.

Touma shot her a look that would have been murderous if she had been visible to anyone else.

“Then why do you do it?”

A pause.

It was brief enough that he could have pretended it meant nothing.

“Because,” Mio said, and when she chose her words carefully the effect was always worse, “you keep insisting you can survive without help, and that has historically been one of your least convincing habits.”

That should have irritated him enough to stay angry.

Instead, it hit too close.

Touma looked away first.

The walk to the station was all ordinary damage. Loud crosswalks. A delivery scooter clipping a curb. Two salarymen arguing with the exhausted ferocity of people who had been awake too long and trusted each other too little. Touma could have pretended, for maybe one block, that his life was a sequence of these things and not the opening of an especially hostile file.

Then the station entrance caught the morning light, and he remembered the lights going out last night.

Not as a picture.

As the shape of his pulse changing.

He stopped again.

“Don’t do that,” Mio said.

“Do what?”

“Freeze in public like the world has personally insulted you.”

“It has.”

“That’s fair, but it is still inconvenient.”

Touma let out a short laugh despite himself. It escaped before he could stop it, which made him hate it and also made the back of his throat ache in a way he did not have a word for.

A convenience store stood beside the station stairway. Its front windows were plastered with summer drink ads and one bright, badly drawn sign promising discounted pastries before noon. Touma had not eaten breakfast. His body, clearly aware that his mind was elsewhere, had decided to become dramatic about it.

He turned toward the shop.

“No,” Mio said.

He ignored her and stepped inside.

The chime over the door played a tune so aggressively cheerful it felt like a personal attack. Cool air hit the back of his neck. The fluorescent lights were too honest. A pair of middle-school girls stood by the freezer case comparing limited-edition sodas with the seriousness of military analysts. An office worker in shirtsleeves was losing a quiet argument with the hot-food shelf.
For one stupid, fragile second, the store looked like a world built entirely out of ordinary inconvenience.

Touma almost resented how badly he wanted that.

The clerk looked up from behind the register with the dead-eyed suspicion of someone who had already seen too many versions of Touma’s face to be impressed by this one. Touma pretended not to notice and took a bottle of tea from the fridge.

“Sugar and caffeine on an empty stomach,” Mio said. “Your commitment to bad sequencing remains inspirational.”

“It’s tea.”

“It is chemically tea,” she said. “Spiritually it is a cry for help.”

That almost got him.
He hated that it almost got him.
The corner of his mouth twitched anyway, and because she was a tyrant with excellent hearing, Mio made a tiny sound of satisfaction like she had just won a point in an argument he had not agreed to have.

He was halfway to the counter when his attention snagged on the display shelf beside the magazines.

A keychain.

Cheap acrylic. Navy-blue star. Tiny silver clasp.

His hand stopped before he could think about it.

The shape was so stupidly specific it felt less like an object than a mistake he had once made and then somehow kept surviving.
Haru had carried the matching one on his school bag for almost a year because he had once declared, with unbearable sincerity, that heroes should not commute alone.

Touma frowned.

Mio went very quiet.

That should have warned him.

He stared at the keychain long enough for the clerk to clear his throat in a way that meant hurry up or leave. Touma reached for the bottle instead, then the keychain, then stopped again because both motions felt wrong.

“Do you know what that is?” Mio asked.

The question landed with a softness that was somehow sharper than her usual mockery.

Touma’s first instinct was to snap that, yes, obviously, it was a keychain, and no, he was not in the mood to be quizzed like a child who had wandered into a philosophy lecture.

But the answer did not arrive.

Not because he didn’t know it.

Because for one alarming second, he knew that he knew it, and then the knowledge shifted sideways like a drawer on broken rails.

His mouth opened.

Nothing came out.

He felt absurdly cold.

The clerk tapped the counter with one finger.

Touma jerked, bought the tea on reflex, and walked out before he could embarrass himself further.

The instant the doors slid shut behind him, he heard Mio inhale sharply.

“You felt that,” he said.

“Yes,” she said, and the word was too fast.

Touma stopped on the station steps. Commuters flowed around him with the polite violence of people who had decided not to care.

“What was that?” he asked.

“Nothing.”

He turned his head toward the empty space where her presence pressed close.

“That was a lie.”

“It was an incomplete answer.”

“Same thing.”

“No,” she said.

The word was flat.

Touma’s grip tightened on the tea until condensation slicked his palm.

“What did you do?”

Mio did not answer right away.

That meant she was deciding how much to tell him, which was almost always the part where things got worse.

Before she could speak, a girl on the sidewalk in front of the station dropped her grocery bag with a curse and bent to chase a rolling apple toward the curb. Touma moved without thinking, caught the apple with his foot, and nudged it back before a bus wheel could turn it into pulp.

The girl looked up, breathless, and immediately looked embarrassed that she had been seen being clumsy.

Touma bent and picked up the bag for her. “Here.”

“Th-thanks,” she said, taking it with both hands.

It was such a tiny normal exchange that it hit him like a bruise.

He was still standing too close. She was still staring at the tea in his hand. Mio was still quiet. Touma found himself suddenly, irrationally aware that he had no idea what expression his face was wearing.

He tried to smile.

The girl smiled back with the sort of relief that means this conversation is over and now no one needs to invent an extra problem.

That should have been easy.

Instead, Touma’s stomach tightened in a way he did not like.

Mio spoke with unnatural gentleness.

“Do you want me to tell you why the keychain mattered?” she asked.

Touma glanced at the bag, at the girl, at the curb, and then at the empty air beside him. He hated that he needed to look at the invisible thing to decide what to do.

“Not here,” he muttered.

“Good,” Mio said. “Because this is the sort of public moment where your brain is likely to make it everyone’s problem.”

“Hey.”

“I’m helping.”

“That is not help.”

“It is if you remember the wrong thing while standing in traffic.”

The girl with the groceries bowed quickly and hurried away before the exchange could become any stranger.

Touma watched her go, then looked at the tea in his hand again.

The keychain image was still there at the edge of his head, not gone, just inaccessible. That was worse than forgetting. Forgetting had clean edges. This felt like something pressing from the other side of a door.

He turned the bottle once between his palms.

Then the memory of the station incident flashed back at him—the small-built teen, the navy-blue shape dangling from his hand, the way his own pulse had jumped when the kid looked at it.

Not a full memory.

A seam.

Touma went cold.

He whispered, “That was Haru’s.”

Mio answered immediately, and now there was something almost clinical in her tone. “Yes.”

“You knew I’d forget it.”

“Yes.”

Touma looked up sharply. “And you didn’t stop me.”

“I tried.”

He heard the defensive note, the tiny sting under it, and got angrier because she sounded like a person catching blame when he did not have the shape to hold it.

“You’re supposed to be monitoring me,” he said.

“I am.”

“Then monitor better.”

A pause.

When Mio spoke again, her voice had gone a degree too bright, which was how she sounded when she had chosen a register he was not supposed to notice.

“I did not expect the anchor to be encoded into a convenience-store object,” she said. “That was a poor design decision on your part.”

Touma barked a laugh, and she cut in immediately, “Do not laugh. I am not done being displeased with you.”

He stared at the empty air. “You’re weirdly personal about this.”

“I am objectively professional.”

“People who are professional do not say things like that.”

“People who are emotional do.”

He felt his ears go hot.

That was a new register. Not snark. Not dry contempt. Something tidier, almost bureaucratic, and somehow more irritating because it sounded like she had moved from mocking him to filing him.

Touma was about to retort when a voice from behind him said, “There you are.”

He turned.

A Union courier stood at the bottom of the station stairs, one hand raised in greeting. Thin build, neutral expression, too much laminated confidence. The sort of person who could make delivery work sound like a moral philosophy.

Touma recognized the envelope in her other hand.

“Late,” he muttered.

“Your schedule changed,” she said. “You didn’t answer the first message.”

Touma grimaced. He had, in fact, seen the first message and then forgotten it existed after the station incident because his brain had become a hostile apartment with the lights off.

Mio’s voice had sharpened.

“Take the envelope,” she said.

Touma narrowed his eyes. “Now you’re giving commands?”

“I am giving logistical advice. Do not make me regret the distinction.”

The courier looked from Touma’s face to the empty air beside his shoulder and then, with visible effort, decided not to ask.

Touma took the envelope.

The courier left.

He tore it open on the step, the paper cracking too loudly in the morning rush.

Inside was a transit key and a folded note with Union’s seal.

RETRIEVAL CONFIRMATION.

STATION STORAGE ANNEX.

DO NOT ENTER ALONE.

Touma stared at it for half a second too long.

Then he laughed, not because it was funny but because he could feel the day trying to bite him and the only way to answer was with his teeth bared.

“Do not enter alone,” he repeated.

Mio’s answer was instant, but this time the joke register was gone completely.

“That,” she said, “is the first thing today I agree with.”

He looked again at the folded note.

Then at the station entrance.

Then at the invisible girl in his skull who knew the shape of his damage better than he did and was beginning, inconveniently, to sound worried.

Touma slid the key into his pocket.

The anchor still nagged at him—small, blue, almost certainly stupidly important. He could feel the edge of a person-shaped absence around it now, and that was somehow more intimate than remembering a face.

His stomach dropped when he realized what that meant.

He was not just losing objects.

He was losing connections to people through them.

The thought made the station seem suddenly too bright.

Mio said, very softly, “Touma.”

He did not answer.

“Do not go chasing the rest of that memory in public.”

He swallowed. “Why not?”

“Because,” she said, and for once there was no snark in it at all, only a thin edge of fear she tried and failed to hide, “I do not know what it will cost if you pull too hard.”

Touma turned his head just enough to feel her presence and not enough to meet whatever expression she was making inside the place he could not see.

That fear sat under his ribs.

It did not have the shape of her usual teasing.

It had the shape of someone recognizing a fracture line and deciding, too late, that she had already crossed it.

Touma clenched the tea bottle until the label creased.

Then he looked toward the station stairwell, where the retrieval errand waited like a bad joke with paperwork.

“Fine,” he said.

Mio did not sound relieved.

That was the worst part.

“Fine,” she repeated.

Touma headed up the steps with the keychain hole in his head, the envelope in his pocket, and the ugly feeling that whatever he had forgotten at the station was not just a name.

It was a thread.

And something was already pulling on it.

### 03 — Union Says Do Not Touch That

By the time Union support finally stopped pretending the situation could be handled with a polite message, Touma had acquired three things: a headache, a stale tea, and the suspicion that everyone in official channels had practiced sounding calm while lying.

He sat in a narrow consultation room with his elbows on his knees and his jacket still damp from the walk in. The room was white in the way that made every human flaw look preemptively documented. A single wall display waited darkly at the far end. Someone had left a plastic cup beside the sink. Touma hated the cup on principle.

Mio had been quiet since the station.

That silence was not soothing.

It was the kind of silence you got from a cat after it had discovered something dead in your shoe.

Touma crossed one ankle over the other and stared at the closed door.

“You could at least tell me what we’re doing here,” he muttered.

“You were informed,” Mio said.

“By a text that read ‘come in now’ like I was about to be audited by God.”

“That does not change the fact of your attendance.”

He huffed a laugh through his nose and regretted it immediately. The pulse in his skull had been a low-grade menace all morning, a reminder that his brain was not in a stable enough state to be allowed unsupervised near a bad decision.

Which, unfortunately, described most of his life.

The door slid open.

The person who walked in was younger than Touma expected, which immediately made him distrust him. Not because youth was suspicious in itself, but because officials and researchers and anyone else with authority always seemed to collect one or two extra years of artificial gravity the moment they entered a room. This guy had none of it.

He wore Union gray with the sleeves rolled up, dark hair tied back badly enough to qualify as an argument, and the kind of expression that said he had stopped trying to make first impressions and had instead settled on accuracy.

He stopped just inside the door and looked at Touma like he was taking measurements.

Touma looked back like he might bite.

The man’s gaze flicked once, almost imperceptibly, to the empty space beside Touma’s shoulder.

Then he said, “You’re worse than the report.”

Touma blinked.

“That’s one way to introduce yourself.”

“It’s not an introduction.” He set a slim tablet on the table and pulled out the chair opposite Touma. “It’s an assessment.”

“Then your assessment is rude.”

“It’s accurate.”

Touma leaned back just enough to make sitting look like a negotiation.

Nico noticed the glance toward the empty air. His mouth twitched like he had almost smiled and then remembered Touma was having a bad day.

“Before we go further,” he said, “I need you to stop me if I start sounding too reassuring. That’s usually a bad sign.”

Touma stared at him.

“That’s the most suspicious thing anybody has ever said to me in a white room.”

“Good. You’re still attentive.”

Touma rubbed the heel of his hand over one eye.

“Who are you?”

“Nico Arata,” the man said. “Union technical liaison on this side of the district. You can call me Nico because if you call me Mister Arata I’ll assume you’re trying to start a fight you are not prepared to finish.”

Touma stared at him.

Nico stared back.

Then, after a beat, Nico’s eyes cut briefly toward the empty air again and softened by one degree.

That was worse.

Touma’s spine went rigid.

“You can see her,” he said.

Nico did not answer immediately.

Which was answer enough.

Touma’s headache sharpened into something hot and personal.

“The hell,” he said.

“Not in the clinical sense,” Nico said. “Not visually, anyway. More like—”

“Don’t say ‘more like.’”

“—more like your interface is taking up enough functional space that I can detect the interference pattern.”

Touma stared.

Mio, in his head, said dryly, “He’s trying to explain you like a machine.”

“Thank you,” Touma muttered under his breath.

Nico’s eyes narrowed. “You were talking to her?”

Touma hated the fact that he immediately answered, “I always am.”

That got a pause.

A real one.

Then Nico slid the tablet across the table and turned it around so Touma could read the opening line.

ADAPTIVE LIMITER SYSTEM: PROTECTIVE Failsafe / COGNITIVE STABILITY REGULATION

Touma snorted once with humorless disbelief.

“Protective,” he repeated. “That’s generous.”

“It’s the label it was given.”

“By who?”

Nico’s expression did not change, but the room somehow got more serious.

“By people who prefer paperwork to ethics.”

Touma let that sit for one second before saying, “That’s still not an answer.”

“No,” Nico said. “It’s a warning.”

Mio went so still in his skull that Touma felt the absence of her commentary like a pressure drop.

He hated that.

He also hated that he was afraid to ask the obvious question.

So he asked it anyway.

“Is this about the implant?”

Nico folded his hands together.

“Yes.”

Touma waited.

Nico waited back.

The silence lasted just long enough to become insulting.

Touma sat forward. “All right, fine. Explain. Start at the part where nobody is pretending this is a normal medical device.”

Nico looked almost relieved to be asked directly.

“Good,” he said. “Then we can skip the fiction.”

He tapped the tablet once, and the display woke to a dense diagram of overlapping threads and warning markers. Touma’s eyes hated it on sight.

“This is not a simple limiter,” Nico said. “It’s an adaptive failsafe bonded to your cognitive profile. Part implant, part artifact-mediated interpretive shell. It was installed to suppress a broader event signature—one that doesn’t read as a thought, exactly, but as the conditions under which thought becomes dangerous.”

Touma’s mouth went dry.

“That is the least understandable explanation I have ever heard.”

“It’s also the most honest one you’re going to get without signing away your remaining patience.”

Touma glared.

Nico did not flinch.

Mio broke the silence from inside him.

“Ask about threshold manipulation,” she said.

Touma blinked.

Nico’s eyes sharpened. “She knows terminology.”

“She knows everything,” Touma said. “I’m the one suffering.”

Nico let that pass, which was annoying in a different way.

Touma pointed at the diagram. “Threshold manipulation?”

“Your implant monitors instability.” Nico glanced at the screen, then at Touma. “It’s supposed to intervene when you cross a certain measured level of cognitive collapse. In theory, that keeps you functional. In practice, it also means the threshold can be spoofed if someone knows how the system interprets stress, conflict, dissociation, and self-model variance—or has access to the same calibration artifact family used to tune it.”

Touma looked between Nico and the tablet.

“So somebody can trick it.”

“Yes.”

“Who?”

Nico’s face went still in a way Touma did not like.

“Someone with enough patience, enough contempt for consent, and access to tooling he was never supposed to keep.”

Touma’s jaw tightened.

He did not need to ask for the name. Kureha was already written all over the shape of the problem like grease.

He looked down at his hands.

The fingers were his. The nails were short because he bit them when he was anxious. His left thumb had a small pale scar from an accident he had half-remembered and then not.
His right hand had already gone, on reflex, to the cheap navy-blue star clipped inside his jacket pocket. He worried the acrylic edge once with his thumb and felt nothing like comfort, only the ugly proof that his body still reached for it before his mind caught up.

For a stupid second he wondered whether the scar would still be there if he tried to remember where it came from too hard.

Mio noticed the thought.

“You’re spiraling,” she said.

“Thank you,” Touma muttered.

“You’re welcome.”

He looked up at Nico. “If it’s a failsafe, what exactly is it failing safe against?”

Nico’s expression hardened.

“That depends on who you ask.”

“That sounds like another lie.”

“It’s institutional ambiguity. Much more expensive.”

Touma would have laughed if the room had not suddenly felt like it was shrinking around his ribs.

Nico rested one hand on the tablet.

“The short version,” he said, “is that your limiter was designed to keep a much larger cognitive event from propagating through you. If the suppression fails, the event doesn’t just hurt you. It can cascade. Memory structure, identity continuity, shared recognition—anything built on stable cognitive reference can be compromised.”

He tapped one corner of the diagram.
A cluster of warning markers expanded into smaller annotations Touma could not fully read before wishing he had not tried.
RECOGNITION INSTABILITY.
ANCHOR LOSS.
LOCAL REFERENCE CASCADE.
There were timestamps beside some of them and clearance tags beside others, as if somebody somewhere had tried to turn this kind of damage into a filing problem.

Touma felt cold spread under his skin.
“Those are real case labels?” he asked.

Nico’s mouth tightened. “Those are the polite names.”

That answer landed worse than if he had said yes.
Because it meant there were impolite names too. Names used in rooms where people discussed this kind of harm often enough to need shorthand for it.

Mio had gone silent again, but not with indifference. Touma could feel the quality of her attention changing in his skull: less like commentary, more like somebody bracing for impact in a seat that had no straps.

Nico zoomed the display out. The threads on-screen reassembled into a shape that looked less like medical telemetry and more like a city map after a bomb threat.
Some paths were marked suppressive.
Some corrective.
Some, in a color Touma immediately distrusted, were labeled continuity salvage.

Touma heard the phrase and hated it on sight.
Salvage was what you did after a wreck.
Salvage was what you called the part where you admitted something had already been destroyed and were now negotiating over what counted as recoverable.

Touma heard the words, but his brain snagged on one phrase.

Shared recognition.

He thought, unwillingly, of the gray-coat man at the station.

Found you.

His stomach turned.

Mio spoke very carefully.

“Ask him what happens if the failsafe is removed.”

Touma did.

Nico did not answer right away.

That was bad.

The kind of bad that arrives dressed as caution.

“When the suppressive architecture is removed,” Nico said at last, “one of three things happens. Best case, the underlying event remains dormant but the subject becomes vulnerable to recurrence. Worst case, you lose the structure supporting the interface.”

Touma’s heart gave a painful little kick.

“What interface?” he asked, though he already knew.

Nico’s gaze slid, for the first time, with obvious intent to the empty air beside Touma.

“The one that is keeping her coherent.”

Touma’s chair legs scraped the floor as he lurched forward. “Meaning what? She dies?”

Nico held up one hand, not quite a stop sign and not quite a comfort. “Meaning we do not know whether the transfer state persists. Meaning she could degrade, disperse, or remain partially embodied with the system unable to stabilize her.”

“That is not better.”

“I didn’t say it was.”

Touma opened his mouth to argue and shut it again when he realized he couldn’t tell whether he was angry at Nico or just at the fact that the room had become a place where his voice could injure the wrong thing.

Inside his skull, Mio was silent.

That scared him more than the words.

Nico watched him carefully, then pushed the tablet a fraction closer.

“There’s one more thing,” he said.

Touma’s eyes narrowed. “Of course there is.”

“If the suppressive architecture comes apart under stress, the same event that damages the interface can reclassify what’s left of it.”

Touma stared. “Speak human.”

Nico exhaled through his nose. “Your system may not just fail to remove her. It may decide she was always part of the contingency structure.”

Touma felt cold all the way through.

“That’s insane.”

“Yes.”

“That means you built a person-shaped trap around me.”

Nico did not answer immediately.

His refusal did.

Touma stood so fast the chair slapped backward into the wall.

The room rang with the impact.

A pair of Union staffers passing the door glanced in, saw the posture of the room, and kept walking.

Touma pointed at the tablet. “No. I’m not doing this if there’s a chance you just erase her.”

Nico did not move.

“Then don’t think of it as erasure,” he said.

Touma laughed once, sharp and ugly. “That’s a disgusting sentence.”

“It is,” Nico said. “And it’s the only reason I’m saying it. If the process works cleanly, she should detach as a stable projection. If it doesn’t—”

“Don’t.”

“—then you need to know what you’re choosing.”

Touma stared at him, breathing too hard.

He should have been furious enough to leave. Instead he felt the stubborn, nauseating weight of the same thing he’d felt on the platform when the kid ran away: the sense that he was already past the point where wanting an easier answer mattered.

Mio still had not spoken.

Touma hated that silence with an almost physical intensity.

He dropped back into the chair, but not before the movement became a statement.

“Tell me the real version,” he said. “No euphemisms.”

Nico looked at him for a long moment.

Then, carefully: “If we remove the failsafe and the wrong structure collapses, you may lose pieces of what makes the current version of her legible to you. She may remain visible. She may not remain knowable. Or she may stay as she is and discover there are new rules she has to obey.”

Touma swallowed.

He hated that the fear arriving now was not abstract. It was not about theory or procedure. It was about Mio’s voice going thin, or wrong, or absent. It was about the shape of a presence becoming a maybe.

The realization hit him a second before he could name it.

Mio was afraid.

Not of the procedure in general.

Of a specific outcome.

Touma turned his head slightly toward the empty air beside his shoulder. “You’ve been scared since we walked in here.”

Mio said nothing.

That was answer enough.

Touma’s throat tightened.

Nico looked between them, quietly alert.

Touma had not expected to be the one recognizing it first. He had expected Mio to keep talking until she found a place to hide the fear in a joke. But now that he was looking for it, he could hear it under the silence: a restraint too hard, a delay too precise, as if she had already made the calculation and hated the result.

He spoke softly, so Nico would not hear the worst of it as clearly.

“You’re afraid the removal doesn’t free you,” Touma said.

Mio answered in a voice so low it barely rose above the hum of the room, “I am afraid of what it calls freedom.”

Touma went still.

That was not a sentence a machine said.

That was a person admitting she had already imagined being changed into something that would still answer when spoken to and no longer mean the same thing.

The room around him felt suddenly very small.

Nico did hear it, and he went careful in that way researchers did when they had just stumbled into an emotion more dangerous than the power.

Touma looked at the tablet again.

The words could not become gentler.

Neither could the choice.

He looked up at Nico and said, “What do I have to do?”

Nico held his gaze for a long second before reaching for the next page of the display.

The hook under all of it sharpened into a clean line: whatever came out of removal, it was already too late to pretend Mio was just a tool, and Touma had just learned that she knew exactly how badly that mattered.

A warning tone chimed once from the console.

Nico’s head snapped toward the door.

Touma followed the motion a heartbeat later, because the room had changed in a way his body understood before his mind did: the air had gone tense, as if somebody on the other side of the wall had just moved with intent.

Then a voice came through the intercom, clipped and unfamiliar.

“Arata. Don’t proceed yet.”

Nico froze.

Touma looked from the door to Nico’s face to Mio’s sudden rigid stillness.

The voice on the intercom continued, almost conversational.

“We have an observer request on the external side. And the system just flagged your patient’s interface as visible.”

Touma’s stomach dropped.

Mio turned her head very slowly toward the closed door.

For the first time since she had appeared, she looked less like a sarcastic miracle and more like someone who had just recognized the shape of the room she used to live in.

Touma swallowed.

Outside the door, someone reached for the handle.

### 04 — Removal Attempt, Worst Outcome

The room they used for the procedure was not a surgical theater.

Touma was grateful for that in exactly the way a man is grateful when the guillotine is replaced with a very sharp stool. It was still a bad place to be. It still contained bright lights, sealed surfaces, and enough sterile equipment to make him suspicious of everyone involved. But it did not contain the theater-grade cruelty of a room that expected blood.

Nico had called it a containment workspace.

Touma had called it a bad sign.

Nico, who apparently respected naming things with honest intent, had not argued.

Mio had not spoken for the last eleven minutes.

Touma would have preferred shouting.

At least shouting had shape.

This silence felt like someone standing very still behind him with a knife and the patience to wait.

He sat on the edge of the padded chair while Nico adjusted the monitor array at the workstation. The equipment arced around Touma in pale bands of light and cable restraint, reading his pulse, his eye movement, the micro-variations in his breathing that he would have denied having if asked.

“Tell me again why I agreed to this,” Touma said.

“Because you are stubborn, frightened, and currently committed to the belief that if the answer hurts less than the question you can call that progress.”

Touma turned his head. “That sounds personal.”

“It is.”

He scowled.

Nico checked a readout and said, “Sit still.”

Touma barked a laugh. “You say that like I’m being difficult on purpose.”

“You are.”

“Fair.”

The room hummed.

There was a second chair to the left of Touma’s, empty except for a folded cable and a set of diagnostic glasses. He kept looking at it despite himself because the emptiness beside him had become a thing with weight.

Mio had manifested once already.

Not enough to be called visible.

Just enough to make the air feel occupied.

Touma hated that his body knew the difference.

Nico moved around behind him. “I need you to hold the stability line steady.”

Touma snorted. “As opposed to what?”

“As opposed to panicking in a way the implant interprets as a structural event.”

“Great. So, normal.”

“That would be ideal, yes.”

Touma lowered his chin and stared at his own knees.

He could feel the limiter behind his skull like a cold coin pressed into skin. Not painful. Worse than painful. Familiar.

His life had been built around not noticing it.

Now he was about to ask someone to rip it out.

The thought made his stomach clench so hard he nearly stood up and fled.

Instead, he heard Mio.

Not with words.

With a sensation.

Warmth under the panic.

A small, brutal kind of clarity.

Understood, she would have called it, if she were in the mood to be infuriatingly precise.

Touma swallowed.

“Are you there?” he whispered.

A pause.

Then, so quietly it felt like she had to cross a distance to answer, “Unfortunately.”

Something in his chest cracked sideways.

He let out a breath that almost became a laugh.

“Don’t do that.”

“Do what?”

“Sound like you’re trying not to leave.”

Mio was silent for two beats.

“That would be because I am not done being useful.”

Touma turned his face away.

The room blurred for one absurd second, not from tears, exactly, but from the sheer effort of keeping his body in one piece when the people around him were discussing whether his mind could be safely disassembled.

Nico came back around to the front, tablet in hand.

“We need to phase the limiter into a partial release,” he said. “Not full removal. We can force a manual decoupling sequence and see whether the interface survives the detachment.”

Touma glared up at him. “See whether she survives the detachment.”

Nico did not contradict him.

That was somehow kinder and worse.

Touma looked away first this time, because if he kept looking at Nico he would start hating him in a way that was too easy.

“Is there a version of this where I don’t feel like an idiot afterward?” Touma asked.

“No.”

“Comforting.”

“It wasn’t meant to be.”

Touma leaned back against the chair and shut his eyes.

The procedure began with a countdown.

He hated countdowns.

They implied the world was being polite enough to warn you.

Nico’s voice cut through the hush. “On my mark, breathe out and hold. Do not fight the first impulse. Let the implant read the distress and normalize it.”

“Normalize it,” Touma repeated.

“Try.”

Mio said, “He means ‘do not explode cognitively.’”

Touma barked a startled laugh right as Nico said, “Three.”

“Don’t distract me,” Touma hissed.

“You were already distracted,” Mio said.

“Two.”

Touma braced his hands against the chair.

The room sharpened.

The hum of the monitors deepened into something that lived under the skin.

“One.”

For the first time in days, Touma became vividly aware that there was a whole architecture of fear inside him that had never needed a name because he had been too busy living inside it.

“Now,” Nico said.

Touma exhaled.

The limiter struck back.

It was not pain.

It was an argument.

Something deep and clinical tried to sort his panic, his resistance, his fear of loss, his fear of being changed, his fear of changing someone else, into a stable line of readable data.

Touma’s vision flashed white at the edges.

He heard himself make a noise that was half choke, half laugh, half insult.

“Still with me?” Nico demanded.

“No,” Touma snapped immediately, because lying was the only thing that kept him from screaming.

Mio’s voice hit like a hand on his shoulder.

Yes, she said.

Hold.

The word landed.

Not as command.

As structure.

Touma clenched his jaw and did exactly what he was told, which was humiliating on principle and necessary in fact.

The monitors stuttered.

Then the room changed.

Not physically.

Cognitively.

The edges of sound thinned. The light in the room developed depth. Touma became aware of a second set of calculations moving under his thoughts like a current under ice.

Nico swore.

Touma tried to turn his head and found, for one terrible second, that the motion of turning was linked to a memory of someone laughing beside him.

Not a face.

Not a name.

Just laughter.

His chest hurt.

The implant pulsed again.

Then something failed.

Not loudly.

Not dramatically.

The way a thread fails when it has been pulled too long.

Touma’s breath stuttered.

The room lurched sideways in his perception and then snapped into a bizarre kind of stillness.

He opened his eyes.

At first he thought he was hallucinating from stress.

Then he saw the shape standing near the empty chair.

Mio.

Not a voice.

Not a pressure.

A girl-shaped hologram, pale and sharp-edged in the white light, with the same dry expression she had always had in his head and the deeply offended posture of someone who had been forced to attend her own birth.

Touma stared.

Mio stared back.

Then Touma made a sound that was not quite human.

“What,” he said.

Mio looked down at herself with immediate suspicion.

“What,” she echoed, sounding for the first time in his life like she had lost the argument with reality.

Touma lurched halfway out of the chair. “No. No, no, no.”

Nico, who had gone very still at the workstation, whispered, “It worked.”

“That is not the word I would choose,” Touma snapped.

Mio lifted one hand and stared at her own fingers.

Her expression changed by a millimeter.

He knew her well enough to see the exact moment she realized she had texture.

“Oh,” she said, and the word was tiny.

Touma looked at Nico, then back at her, then back at Nico again.

“Why is she here?” he demanded.

Nico’s face had drained of color.

“I don’t know,” he said, which was the first honest thing he’d said since walking into the room. “I expected decoupling, not manifest anchoring. The interpretive shell should have collapsed or detached. Instead it pushed outward and re-housed her reference state.”

Touma pointed at Mio, who was now glaring at the air over her own palm like she was considering starting a war with physics.

“Manifest anchoring,” he repeated. “You just made her visible.”

“I didn’t make her visible.” Nico’s voice sharpened with alarm. “The system has re-housed her reference state in a perceptible projection. That’s not normal.”

“Nothing about this has been normal since the station,” Touma said.

Mio looked up at him.

Her eyes, impossible as ever, were now a little too bright in the room’s hard light.

“The upside,” she said, with painful composure, “is that I am no longer trapped in your skull.”

Touma stared at her.

His relief hit first so hard it was almost anger.

Then the anger followed.

Then the horror.

He took one step toward her before remembering that her body, if body was the word, was made of something the room had not been designed to handle.

He stopped.

Mio noticed that immediately.

Her mouth tightened.

“You can touch the chair,” she said flatly.

“That is not the point.”

“It is absolutely the point.”

Touma looked at her, at the outline of her shoulders, at the fact of her standing there where she had only ever been voice and pressure and mockery and impossible protection.

The room should have felt miraculous.

Instead it felt wrong in ten different directions at once.

The air around Mio had a faint particulate shimmer, like a projector trying to remember the shape of dust. The overhead lights flickered across her and made her look too sharp, all edges and null spaces. When she shifted her weight, there was a half-beat delay before the floor registered it. Not soundless exactly, but wrong in the social way silence can be wrong when a person is standing too close.

Touma became aware, with miserable immediacy, that everyone in the room was now reacting to her.

Nico had gone from researcher to witness.

One of the support staff outside the glass had stopped walking and was staring as if he’d just seen a lab instrument grow a pulse.

Mio saw him see her.

Her expression changed.

Not fear. Not yet.

A tiny flare of offended dignity, quickly hidden.

Touma recognized it as the kind of thing she would have disguised in his head by making fun of him. Now she had nowhere to put it.

It made his chest ache.

Nico finally found his voice. “Do not move anything unnecessary.”

“Was that to me or her?” Touma asked.

“Yes.”

Touma stared.

Mio looked over at Nico with open disdain. “That was a useless instruction.”

Nico, to his credit, did not take the bait. He pointed at the readout instead. “Your projection is unstable. Touma, if you’re going to panic, do it slowly.”

Touma let out one short, disbelieving laugh that came out sounding like a choke.

“You say that like I have a schedule for it.”

“I’m trying to establish one.”

Mio’s gaze flicked to Touma, then to the empty chair, then to the cable hanging loose from the monitor array.

She reached toward the cable with obvious caution.

Her hand passed through it by a fraction before the system caught up and the cable’s edge shimmered against her palm.

She jerked back.

Touma saw it and felt the first real spike of panic since the procedure started.

“You okay?” he blurted.

Mio blinked at him as if the question had surprised her more than the procedure.

“Do not sound so worried,” she said automatically.

“That wasn’t the question.”

“It was close enough.”

No. It wasn’t.

But he had already learned that his body, at least, was capable of worrying in front of her without getting permission from his embarrassment.

Touma took another careful step and stopped himself again.

Mio noticed the stop and the not-stop and something in her face shuttered.

Oh, he thought. She’s scared of me hitting the wrong boundary.

The thought landed before he could stop it.

Mio’s eyes snapped to his.

Touma realized he had said it out loud only a second later.

“I’m not—” he started.

“I know,” she said too fast.

That was worse.

Touma rubbed a hand over his mouth and looked away.

The room kept making tiny, humiliating noises around them: the monitor tone, the hum of the light rail system in the wall, the soft rattle of a tray no one had meant to leave loose.

Nico had turned one screen toward himself and was scanning data faster now, all the procedural calm of a man trying not to witness a catastrophe while standing in the middle of it.

Touma could feel the moment splitting into two tracks: one where he focused on the procedure, and one where he focused on the fact that Mio was visible and therefore had become part of the room’s social geometry.

He hated the geometry immediately.

Mio had always occupied his space without being asked. Now she occupied it visibly, and the room had to decide where to put her relative to tables, chairs, and people who did not know whether to call this a miracle or a containment failure.

A staffer outside the glass whispered something to another staffer.

The second one flinched.

Mio heard them both.

Touma saw it in the way her jaw tightened.

He also saw something else: fear before anger, brief and involuntary, the way a person reacts when being noticed threatens to become being classified.

He knew that fear.

It had not looked like that in the station. It had looked like irritation and competence and a voice insisting he keep moving.

Now it was visible.

Touma’s throat tightened.

“Mio,” he said softly.

She did not answer right away.

He forced himself to say it anyway. “You can stand here.”

Her eyes narrowed. “That was not helpful.”

“I’m getting there.”

“Do it faster.”

There she was. That little edge. The one that let her pretend she had not just become the most vulnerable thing in the room.

Touma looked at her and then at the empty space around her outline.

He wanted, absurdly, to reach for her shoulder the way he would have if she had still been a voice. The instinct nearly knocked him off balance.

Instead he asked, “Can I stand closer?”

Mio went utterly still.

The room seemed to hold its breath with her.

Touma realized, too late, that the question had hit too much at once: proximity, touch, choice, the fact that he was asking at all.

She stared at him as if he had just done something dangerous and kind in the same motion.

Then, very carefully, “Yes,” she said.

Touma moved one step.

Mio did not move away.

It was so small a thing that it felt huge.

The projection shimmered once at the edges, adjusting to the change in distance like the system was trying to decide whether the room had become more dangerous or merely more intimate.

Touma stopped half a handspan short of her shoulder.

He let the space stay there.

Mio stared at the gap, then at his face.

For a second her usual expression almost failed entirely.

Then it returned, weaker than before.

“Don’t look so pleased with yourself,” she muttered.

Touma laughed under his breath, relief and terror tangled together.

Nico cleared his throat from the workstation. “Good. You are both alive. I’d appreciate it if that remained true long enough for me to finish identifying what just happened.”

Touma turned just enough to glare at him.

Mio beat him to it.

“Your timing is appalling,” she said.

“I’m aware.”

“No, you are not.”

Nico rubbed one temple, briefly abandoning professionalism to the strain of it. “For the record, this is the least normal successful procedure I have ever seen.”

Touma barked a short laugh.

Then the support staffer outside the glass took one step backward and knocked into the doorframe hard enough to rattle the panel.

Everyone looked.

He had seen Mio.

That was impossible enough on its own.

But his face had changed in a way Touma didn’t like at all: recognition with dread under it, the look of someone seeing a shape they had been warned about in advance.

Nico noticed it too.

His voice flattened. “You recognize her.”

The staffer swallowed.

Touma felt the room contract around that answer.

Mio’s projection flickered once, as if the system had heard the word and objected.

Touma looked at her, then at the staffer, then back at Nico.

The procedure had not finished.

It had only moved the problem into the open.

And now somebody else in the building knew exactly what kind of wrong had just stepped out of his skull.

### Interstitial 04.5 — Union Classification Dossier

*Packet-visible Union dossier / support-file interstitial between Chapters 4 and 5.*

Touma had started to hate the phrase *for your safety*.

That was mostly because it always arrived dressed up as procedure.

Procedure was the polite face of something that wanted to touch you without admitting it wanted to touch you. Procedure was how frightened adults made their fear sound like a form. Procedure was the reason Touma was currently sitting in a chair that did not belong to him, in a room that had too much light and too little warmth, while a Union clerk with immaculate nails and a voice like a stapler read his life out loud as if it were a shipping manifest.

Nico sat to Touma’s left, expression carefully neutral in the way that meant he was one syllable away from punching a wall.

Mio sat to Touma’s right.

Which would have been funny, if she did not still look like a bad omen wearing a girl’s shape.

The Union clerk did not look at Mio directly for more than a second. That somehow made it worse.

Touma had expected a scientist. Or a soldier. Or at least someone with the decency to seem alarmed.

Instead they had sent a bureaucrat.

The woman adjusted her glasses and continued, “This meeting is a confirmation of emergent classification pressure arising from post-containment manifestation. We are not here to assign culpability. We are here to determine exposure category, handling rules, and reporting pathway.”

Touma stared at her.

Nico said, very quietly, “That is Union language for ‘we saw the problem and would like it to stop being our problem.’”

Mio muttered, “Charming.”

Touma hissed, “Don’t encourage them.”

The clerk kept reading. “Subject Touma Shihara has already been logged under unstable cognitive anomaly risk due to prior limiter intervention and threshold irregularity. Prior to this incident, the system was considered sealed, masked, and controllable. Following manifestation, the system is now visible enough to trigger secondary classification review.”

Touma rubbed both hands over his face.

There it was.

Not *monster*.

Not *dangerous*.

*Review*.

The word had the same shape as a knife hidden in paperwork.

“Secondary classification,” Touma repeated through his fingers. “That sounds like you’re about to put a sticker on me.”

“Not a sticker,” the clerk said. “A protocol designation.”

“That is somehow worse.”

“It is more accurate.”

Mio turned her head, eyes narrowed. “What designation.”

The clerk finally looked at her. It was a brief, professional glance, but Touma still felt his skin crawl.

“Visible auxiliary cognition,” she said.

Mio’s mouth flattened.

Touma jerked upright. “She is not an auxiliary.”

The clerk did not flinch. “That response is noted.”

“What response?” Touma snapped.

“The protective one.”

Touma shut his mouth.

That was too neat. Too clinical. Too embarrassing.

The clerk made a mark on her pad anyway, and Touma had the unpleasant feeling that his blood pressure had become a bullet point.

Nico leaned forward just enough to ask, “You’re classifying the manifestation instead of suppressing it?”

“We are classifying before deciding whether suppression is justified.”

Mio gave him a look that could have stripped paint.

“Wonderful,” she said. “We’re being ethical in the exact way a blade is ethical.”

The clerk ignored that too. “Because the manifestation is cognitively legible, and because it is linked to a known negator containment structure, Touma Shihara is to remain under observation-level handling until further notice.”

Touma blinked. “Handling level?”

Nico muttered, “That one’s going to annoy you.”

“It already does.”

The clerk flipped the page. “Handling level seven, with a single concrete restriction effective immediately.”

Touma felt the room go very still.

He hated that part most.

The quiet before they told him what he was not allowed to do.

“What restriction?” he asked.

The clerk looked at him over the top of her glasses. “Touma Shihara and the manifested entity are not to remain out of direct Union sightline for more than thirty seconds while inside an unsecured facility or transit space.”

Touma stared.

Then he laughed once, sharply and without humor.

“That is insane.”

“Thirty seconds,” the clerk repeated, like she was helping a child count coins.

“Why?”

“Because after the manifestation, your limiter system no longer guarantees the same containment behavior when you cannot be visually confirmed.”

Touma turned to Nico. “Tell me this is a joke.”

Nico looked pained. “I wish it was.”

Mio crossed her arms. “Let me guess. If I disappear from sight, the system assumes I’ve collapsed into something worse.”

The clerk’s pause was answer enough.

Touma made a noise that did not become a word.

“Hold on,” he said. “You’re telling me I need a babysitter because my girl-shaped problem might become a bigger girl-shaped problem if someone blinks?”

Mio’s head snapped toward him. “Your girl-shaped problem?”

“That is not what I meant.”

“It is exactly what you said.”

Nico shut his eyes for a second like a man praying for death.

The clerk cleared her throat with the delicate authority of someone who had filed worse than this and would file again. “The rule is not negotiable. If the projection is not directly confirmable, any anomalous behavior will be treated as a containment breach until assessed.”

Touma put both hands flat on the table.

“This is because you think she’s a hazard.”

“It is because we do not yet know whether she is a hazard.”

Mio smiled without warmth. “That is the sort of sentence that means ‘yes.’”

Touma looked at Nico. “You knew about this?”

Nico’s silence was immediate, which was rude.

Touma narrowed his eyes. “You knew.”

“I knew they would want a confirmation window,” Nico said. “I did not know they’d make it a sightline rule.”

“That is not better.”

“No, it is not.”

The clerk tapped the page again. “There is also a reporting requirement.”

Touma closed his eyes.

Of course there was.

“Say it.”

“Any time the manifested entity appears outside the containment workspace, the nearest Union contact is to receive a visual confirmation record within fifteen minutes.”

Touma opened one eye. “A photo.”

“A confirmation record.”

“That’s a photo.”

“It may include a photo.”

Touma turned to Nico. “You are going to let them make me send pictures of her like she’s contraband.”

Nico gave him a look. “I am going to let them do a lot of things I don’t like because the alternative is them deciding to detain you both.”

That landed.

Touma hated that it landed.

Mio’s posture changed a fraction. Not less defensive. More careful.

Touma noticed because he was starting to notice too much.

The clerk said, “The public language is ‘controlled support arrangement.’ The private language is ‘do not let the unstable thing out of sight where we cannot measure it.’”

Mio leaned back in her chair, eyes cold. “You people really do love sounding civilized while holding a leash.”

The clerk made another mark. “Noted: manifestation exhibits independent hostility.”

Touma sat up so fast his chair screeched.

“No,” he said. “Don’t do that.”

The clerk looked at him.

Touma hated that he was flushing. Hated that the heat was crawling up his neck. Hated that the room contained three adults and somehow he was the one being embarrassed into visible distress.

“Don’t write her like that,” he said.

“Then provide a less hostile demonstration.”

Mio barked a short laugh.

Touma glared at her. “Not helping.”

“You are making a remarkable amount of effort to be humiliating in public,” she said. “I’m simply observing the craft.”

Nico pressed two fingers to the bridge of his nose.

The clerk set down her pad. “Final note. The thirty-second sightline rule exists for a reason. Your prior incident, Touma Shihara, showed that when the system is forced to function without confirmed external reference, the limiter’s stabilizing logic can be repurposed by an outside operator. If the projection and the host are separated from observation long enough, we may lose the ability to distinguish containment from escalation.”

Touma frowned. “Outside operator.”

Nico’s jaw tightened just slightly.

Touma felt it before he understood it.

“You mean Kureha.”

The clerk did not answer directly, which was answer enough.

Mio’s expression went unreadable in a way Touma already disliked.

“So,” Touma said slowly, “if I want to go to the bathroom, I need to have an audience.”

“Yes,” the clerk said.

Touma stared.

Then, very quietly, he said, “You are kidding.”

“I am not.”

Mio looked at the ceiling. “This is the worst day of my life and I am a hologram.”

Touma made a strangled sound and covered his face.

Nico, traitor that he was, said, “For what it’s worth, the bathroom rule is temporary.”

Touma dropped his hands. “That was not a comforting thing to say.”

“It wasn’t meant to be comforting.”

The clerk stood, smoothing her jacket with the kind of care that suggested she believed order could save her from the shame of her own paperwork. “You will receive written guidance. You will not leave the facility without a sightline escort. You will not allow the manifested entity to wander out of visual range. You will report deviations immediately. If this is not followed, Union response will escalate.”

Touma looked at her. “Escalate how?”

The clerk’s expression did not change.

“That depends on what stops being visible first.”

Silence.

Then Nico stood too quickly, knocking his chair back a little. “All right. That’s enough. We have the rule. You can go now.”

The clerk inclined her head and left with the smooth satisfaction of someone who had just made a problem more legible and therefore, in her mind, more humane.

The door shut.

For two full seconds nobody spoke.

Touma stared at the empty doorway, then at the spot where Mio’s hand rested on the table, then at Nico.

“You were going to tell me they’d do this.”

“I was going to tell you they’d want control measures.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“No,” Nico said. “It isn’t.”

Mio looked down at her own fingers, then at Touma. “You’re not angry at me.”

Touma snorted. “I’m absolutely angry at her.”

“That was not what I asked.”

He looked away.

That was the worst part.

Not the rule.

Not the photo.

Not the sightline escort or the way the Union had turned her into a confirmable risk.

The worst part was that they had named the thing he had been feeling since the procedure: the need to keep Mio visible.

Not because it was romantic.

Not because it was safe.

Because if he let her slip out of sight for too long, someone else might decide she had become a category instead of a person.

Touma pushed back from the table and stood.

The room felt too small for what had just been said in it.

“So what now?” he asked.

Nico looked at him, then at Mio, then at the paperwork still open on the table. “Now,” he said carefully, “you start learning what it costs to keep her in the room.”

Mio’s expression changed.

Not soft.

Not pleased.

Just real.

And Touma hated, with a hot and humiliating certainty, that the answer had landed in his chest like a warning and a promise at the same time.

### 05 — Visible Problem

Touma had imagined a lot of bad mornings in his life.

He had imagined waking up to a broken window.
He had imagined waking up to police outside.
He had imagined waking up to the sort of silence that meant somebody had already made a decision about him.

He had not imagined waking up to Mio standing in the corner of his room with her arms folded and her expression exactly as offended as it had always been, because that would have required him to admit, in advance, that there were worse things than her voice in his head.

She was there anyway.

Not a voice.
Not a pressure at the base of his skull.
Not a hiss of commentary timed to ruin his dignity.

Mio, lit wrong by morning and edged in the faint shimmer of a projection that still had not decided whether it belonged to the room, stood on the cheap rental carpet of the safehouse bedroom like the world had finally made a clerical error large enough to become visible.

Touma sat up so fast he banged his elbow against the wall.

“Don’t do that,” he muttered.

Mio tilted her head. “Do what?”

“Be there.”

“That is not a realistic request.”

He groaned and flopped back onto the futon. The ceiling was still the same suspicious beige. The lamp was still broken. The safehouse still smelled faintly like dust, instant coffee, and the cheap disinfectant Nico had used to make the place feel medically secure without actually improving anything.

Mio looked around the room with the intense suspicion of someone checking a trap for hidden teeth.

Touma watched her do it and had the deeply unhelpful thought that she looked more alive than she had when she was only audible.

That thought made his face hot.

“Stop staring,” she said.

“I’m not staring.”

“You are absolutely staring.”

“I’m trying to figure out if you’ll vanish if I blink.”

Mio gave him a flat look. “If I do, it will be because I finally developed taste.”

Touma huffed a laugh before he could stop himself.

The laugh made the whole room feel less stable.

That was the problem now. There was no longer any safe layer between the strange part of his life and the rest of it. The thing that had once lived inside his head and insulted him in private now occupied space. Space had rules. Space had witnesses. Space had consequences.

And Touma, who had spent most of his life hiding his weirdness behind being difficult and unapproachable, had discovered that the universe could be much crueler than social embarrassment if it wanted to be.

He reached for the blanket. His hand passed through her knee by accident when he moved too quickly.

Mio jerked back. “Warn me before you do that.”

Touma froze. “I didn’t mean to.”

“That’s what makes it worse.”

He stared at her.

Then she looked away first, which was somehow more alarming than if she had kept glaring.

Touma swung his legs over the side of the futon and sat up. “Can you feel anything?”

Mio’s eyes narrowed. “That is a broad question.”

“Don’t start.”

“I’m trying to decide whether you mean physically or emotionally.”

Touma buried his face in his hands. “I hate that you can do that now.”

“Do what?”

“Act like a person who understands me too well.”

A pause.

Mio’s expression shifted in the smallest way. Not quite softer. Not quite sad. Something more guarded than either.

“That was always the case,” she said.

Touma lowered his hands.

He wanted to argue with her. It would have been easier to argue.

Instead, because his life apparently hated ease, the memory of the procedure came back in pieces: Nico’s voice, the ring of monitors, the cold pressure behind his skull when the limiter began to separate, and the moment the room had not just seen Mio but accepted that it had to do something with her.

He rubbed at the back of his neck.

“You know,” he said, “I was expecting ‘death’ or ‘explosion.’ Not this.”

“This,” Mio said, looking down at her own hand, “is also objectionable.”

She flexed her fingers once.

Touma watched the movement with a stupid, helpless amount of focus.

There was a faint lag between intention and result, like a thought that had to ask the room for permission before becoming motion. The projection caught the light in soft bands, not quite transparent, not quite solid, and every time she shifted her weight the outline of her body shimmered at the edges in a way that made Touma think of bad signal and unstable passwords.

He hated that he could see her fear now.

Not because it made him dislike her.

Because it meant he could no longer pretend he had only been dealing with a voice.

Voices could be snide, invasive, annoying, and still remain safely abstract if you wanted them to. A visible Mio had shoulders that tightened when something unexpected happened. A visible Mio took a breath before answering. A visible Mio had to decide where to put her hands when the room got crowded.

A visible Mio could be ignored.

That thought made him angry enough to stand up.

Mio immediately lifted her chin. “If you are about to blame me for physics, I will be very disappointed.”

“I’m not blaming you.”

“Good.”

“I’m blaming the universe.”

“Reasonable.”

Touma ran a hand through his hair and started pacing in the small strip of floor between the futon and the kitchenette. The safehouse had been designed for short-term concealment, which apparently meant it contained one bedroom, a single narrow window, and exactly enough bad furniture to create ankle hazards in dim light.

Mio watched him pace.

He could feel it.

Being watched by her used to mean feeling judged in the privacy of his own skull. Being watched by her now meant he kept accidentally glancing toward her because his body had not yet learned how to stop treating her like the center of the room.

That was a problem.

Not a tactical one.

A social one.

Which, unfortunately, was somehow worse.

The door opened.

Touma jerked so hard he nearly hit the kitchenette counter.

Nico leaned in with a tablet in one hand and the look of a man who had spent the last hour deciding whether this counted as a miracle, a disaster, or a lawsuit. “You’re both awake.”

Touma stared at him. “You knew she could do that?”

“No.”

“That sounded too fast.”

“That is because the answer is no and I am tired.”

Mio folded her arms. “You are always tired.”

Nico looked directly at her. “You are the first problem in this room that has ever talked back at me from outside a closed system.”

Mio raised one eyebrow. “Then you have lived a sheltered life.”

Touma covered his face with one hand.

Nico stepped fully into the room and, very carefully, did not react to the fact that he was now sharing space with Mio as though that were the most normal thing in the world. He was trying too hard to be calm, which meant he was absolutely not calm.

Touma had never seen him look this close to being rattled.

That made Touma feel marginally better.

Then Nico’s eyes flicked once, not quite to Mio and not quite past her, and Touma remembered the stupid rule from yesterday—the one that said they had to stay visible, confirmed, observed, because apparently even being weird now came with a timer.

So of course Nico was standing in the doorway like he was trying not to let the room drift out of witness range.

Great. Even the embarrassment had operational procedure.

Nico held up the tablet. “The limiter is stable. For the moment. That doesn’t mean the projection is stable.”

“Projection,” Touma repeated.

Mio’s eyes narrowed. “Do not call me that like I’m a school presentation.”

Nico ignored that. “Your system is still interpreting her as part of the same control architecture. The visible form is a side effect.”

“Side effect,” Touma echoed. “That’s a strange word to use while looking at a person.”

Nico’s expression sharpened. “I am looking at a person. That’s why I’m being precise.”

Touma didn’t like that answer, which was probably because it sounded more honest than comforting.

Mio’s gaze flicked from Nico to Touma. “He’s right.”

Touma stared at her. “You don’t get to take his side.”

“I didn’t take his side. I took the side where we continue existing.”

“That’s not comforting either.”

“It wasn’t meant to be.”

Nico exhaled through his nose and tapped the tablet. “The important part is that this means other systems may be able to register her now.”

Touma froze.

Mio’s expression did not change, but her shoulders went tight.

“Other systems,” Touma said carefully, “as in people?”

“As in cameras, biometric scanners, active recognition filters, maybe some humans if the conditions are bad enough.”

“Bad enough for who?”

Nico gave him a flat look.

Touma realized the answer before it came.

“For you.”

That was a very rude way to wake up.

He looked at Mio, and she looked away too quickly.

Touma’s stomach sank.

“Wait,” he said. “You’re worried about people seeing her.”

“I’m worried about people classifying her,” Nico said. “Seeing is the harmless version.”

Mio made a small, almost offended noise. “Harmless is an ambitious word for this group.”

Nico nodded once. “Exactly. Which is why I need both of you to understand that you cannot act normally until we know what the projection is leaking.”

Touma barked a laugh. “Normally? We’re past that by several crimes.”

Mio looked at him. “You say that like you didn’t try to remove me from your nervous system less than an hour ago.”

Touma pointed at her. “Do not phrase it like that in front of him.”

Nico, infuriatingly, did not blink. “I already knew.”

“That is not the point.”

“It’s a point.”

Touma made a noise of despair and sat down hard on the edge of the futon.

The mattress creaked under his weight.

Mio’s eyes tracked the movement, then the exact spot where he had sat, and Touma had the sudden terrible realization that she was worried about whether she could share space without being treated as a visual anomaly.

That thought hit him harder than the embarrassment.

He looked up at her. “Sit down.”

Mio blinked. “Excuse me?”

“Sit. If you’re going to stand there looking like a haunted projector, at least use the chair.”

“I do not look haunted.”

“You absolutely do.”

She glared at him, then at the chair, then at Nico as if daring him to comment.

Nico, wisely, pretended not to exist.

Mio took one careful step and sat on the chair by the desk.

The chair made a tiny squeak.

Touma watched her settle and felt a completely unjustified surge of relief that the furniture had not rejected her.

Mio noticed, of course.

“Don’t make that face,” she said.

“What face?”

“The face where you look like you’ve just survived something.”

Touma looked away. “Maybe I have.”

The room went quiet enough for the air conditioner to become noticeable.

Touma hated that quiet.

He hated that it contained the shape of all the things he had not asked yet.

The joke version of this situation would have been easy. He could have insulted her. She could have insulted him back. Nico could have pretended this was all a technical issue. They could have stacked embarrassment until it became almost funny.

And that was part of what kept happening, because embarrassment was the only thing any of them knew how to use when a situation became too intimate to survive directly.

But there was another thing sitting under the jokes now.

Fear.

Mio’s fear wasn’t loud. It never had been. But Touma could see it now in the way she kept checking the room when she thought nobody was looking.

She was afraid of being perceived wrong.

He knew that fear. He knew it in the way he knew cold metal against skin and the feeling of saying the wrong thing in front of someone who could turn it into a life sentence.

He just hadn’t expected it to look so much like embarrassment when it stood up and wore a face.

Touma rubbed the back of his neck and tried to think of something normal to say.

“Can you eat?” he asked.

Nico looked up instantly. Mio blinked.

Touma regretted it at once.

“I mean—” he started.

“I know what you mean,” Mio said, and there was a dangerous softness in her voice now.

Touma shut up.

Mio looked down at her own hand again, more slowly this time. “I don’t know yet.”

He stared at her.

That answer should not have mattered so much.

It did anyway.

Because it meant she had a body-shaped set of unknowns. It meant the world had become capable of confusing her in ways he could not immediately solve. It meant there were going to be moments where she needed something and neither of them would know whether the answer was yes.

Touma hated how quickly his brain tried to inventory solutions.

He also hated how badly he wanted to take her hand.

The impulse came with so much force that he nearly stood up just from the effort of not acting on it.

Mio saw the movement in his posture and stiffened.

That made him stop.

“Oh,” she said quietly.

“What?”

“You were about to ask.”

Touma’s ears went hot. “Ask what?”

“If you can touch me.”

He went silent.

Nico, who had the decency to look away, said, “I am going to check the hallway.”

Touma pointed at him without looking. “No, you are not.”

“I am.”

“You’re leaving because you’re uncomfortable.”

“Yes.”

“That’s cowardly.”

“That’s mature.”

Nico opened the door anyway and slipped out before Touma could find a comeback sharp enough to justify a homicide.

The door shut.

The room became smaller immediately.

Touma wished very strongly that Nico had not done that.

Mio sat very still in the chair. She had the look of someone trying not to admit she had been waiting for the room to empty.

That was bad.

Touma set both hands on his knees. “I was going to ask if it hurt.”

Mio blinked at him.

He pressed on before he could lose the nerve. “I mean when I touch you. Or when things touch you. Or when the room does that weird half-glitch thing.”

Her expression changed by a fraction.

There it was again. The tiniest crack in the armor.

“I don’t know,” she said.

Touma let out a breath.

“That’s not good.”

“No,” Mio said. “It isn’t.”

He looked at her, really looked.

She was trying too hard to hold herself in a shape that still made sense to her. That was the thing no one ever said about people who were scared: they often looked most composed when they were actively trying not to fall apart.

Mio’s fingers twitched once against the chair arm.

Touma noticed immediately.

She noticed him noticing.

“You are making that face again,” she said.

“What face?”

“The one where you think feeling bad is the same as being useful.”

He stared at her.

“That’s an unfairly specific insult.”

“It is an accurate one.”

Touma almost smiled and then didn’t, because the whole situation was still absurd enough to be funny in the worst possible way.

He dragged a hand down his face.

“Okay,” he said. “We need ground rules.”

Mio looked suspicious immediately. “That sounds like the beginning of a nightmare.”

“It’s the beginning of me not tripping over you in the hallway and pretending that’s fine.”

“I did not ask to be visible.”

“I know.”

The bluntness of the answer seemed to catch her off guard.

Touma took the chance before his courage ran out.

“I know you didn’t ask,” he said more quietly. “I know you’re not doing this on purpose.”

Mio looked down.

That tiny movement did more to him than any argument would have.

He looked away first, because if he kept staring he was going to make the kind of expression she would mock for the next week.

“Rule one,” he said. “No surprise touching.”

Mio snorted once. “That is not a rule. That is basic decency.”

“Fine. Rule one is you don’t make fun of me when I forget the obvious.”

“Then I will need a stronger rule.”

“Rule two,” Touma said, “you tell me if something hurts.”

Mio went very still.

Touma hated how much that mattered.

“I don’t want to be a problem,” she said, and it came out too fast to be casual.

Touma looked at her.

There it was. The fear under the comedy. The one she kept trying to bury under sarcasm and precision and that infuriating habit of acting like she only existed if she was currently performing a function.

He swallowed.

“You are already a problem,” he said.

Mio’s mouth twitched like she wanted to hit him with a chair.

He held up one hand. “I mean that in the normal way. The way people are problems when they exist near each other and start affecting each other’s decisions. You know. Humanly.”

“That is a terrible explanation.”

“It’s true, though.”

She looked at him for a long second.

Then, very softly, “Touma.”

He stiffened.

She rarely used his name without a bite in it. Hearing it like that made him feel exposed in a way he didn’t have words for.

“What?” he asked.

Mio’s gaze shifted to the floor, then back up. “If I say something is wrong, you will listen?”

Touma answered too quickly. “Yes.”

She studied him.

He realized, with a sick little lurch, that she did not entirely believe him.

That was fair.

He had spent too much of his life bluffing and too much of his relationship with her insisting she was the annoying part of a survival system instead of the reason he had kept surviving inside it.

Touma forced himself to keep eye contact.

“Yes,” he repeated. “I’ll listen.”

Mio’s expression shifted.

It was not trust yet.

It was the beginning of wanting to believe trust might be less dangerous than it looked.

That was almost harder to see.

The door opened again before Touma could think of anything smart to say.

This time it wasn’t Nico.

It was the support tech from the adjoining hall, a young man with a clipboard and the immediate face of someone who had just walked into the wrong kind of miracle.

He stopped dead when he saw Mio.

Touma’s entire body went tight.

The tech’s eyes widened. “Uh—”

Mio looked at him.

For a second, she looked exactly like the thing Touma had feared and wanted and protected and accidentally brought into being.

Visible.

The tech’s mouth worked once. Then he pointed, with the helpless horror of a person whose instincts had all failed at once.

“Who is that?”

Mio went perfectly still.

Touma heard the blood rush in his ears.

The tech looked from Mio to Touma and back again, already building the wrong story in real time.

Mio’s shoulders tightened.

Touma knew that posture now.

It was the posture of someone trying not to be classified.

Touma stood up.

Too fast.

The tech flinched.

Touma did not care.

“That,” he said, and even he could hear how bad that sounded, “is not your business.”

The tech stared.

Mio shut her eyes for half a second.

Touma saw the embarrassment hit her before the fear did, and he hated the universe for making those two things arrive together.

The tech looked at the empty hallway behind him, then back at Touma, and his expression changed into something much worse than confusion.

Recognition.

Not of Mio.

Of gossip.

Touma knew the moment it happened because the young man’s face did that tiny, doomed shift people get when they realize they have just stepped into a story and may never escape being part of it.

“Oh,” the tech said, with the exact tone of someone who had already decided there would be a version of this anecdote later. “I—right. Sorry. I just needed to—”

“Leave,” Touma said.

The tech left.

The door shut.

The room was quiet for one long, horrible beat.

Then Mio, still staring at the doorway, said, in a voice so flat it almost counted as a scream, “That was public.”

Touma pressed both hands to his face.

“Yes.”

“That was visible.”

“Yes.”

“That was humiliating.”

“Yes.”

He heard her inhale once.

Then, because she was Mio and she could not let a catastrophic emotional event go by without making him suffer for it, she added, “I hope you enjoyed your dramatic reveal.”

Touma let out a noise that was halfway between a laugh and a groan.

“I hate you.”

“Incorrect.”

He lowered his hands and looked at her. She was trying so hard to look annoyed that the fear underneath it became even clearer.

Touma felt his own embarrassment sharpen into something protective.

Too many eyes.

Too many people.

Too much room for this to become a thing.

A rumor.

A weird story.

A problem.

He hated that it was already too late for the first two.

Mio folded her arms. “What do we do if people start asking?”

Touma stared at her.

He had been expecting sarcasm. Maybe even panic.

Not that.

Not the practical question.

The one that meant she was already thinking about the shape of the future embarrassment, because if she did not think about it first then somebody else would decide what it meant.

Touma’s throat tightened.

“We lie,” he said automatically.

Mio looked at him with a disgust so sincere it almost qualified as affection. “That was your plan?”

“I am open to revisions.”

“Badly.”

“You have no idea how badly.”

She looked at the floor for a second and then back up, and there it was again: the tiny, stubborn expression she wore when fear tried to occupy the same space as humor and she refused to let it win cleanly.

Touma had seen that expression in his head so many times that seeing it on her face now felt like getting punched by a memory he hadn’t earned yet.

Mio said, “If I am going to be seen, I refuse to be seen as a mistake.”

Touma went still.

The room, the safehouse, the stupid hallway outside the stupid door—everything narrowed around that sentence.

Because that was it, wasn’t it.

Not the visible problem.

The real problem.

If she was visible, then other people would start treating her like a question that needed answering.

Was she real?
Was she an implant side effect?
Was she his symptom?
Was she useful?
Was she safe?
Was she a threat?

Touma looked at her and felt the embarrassment burn off into something fiercer.

“No,” he said.

Mio blinked.

He stepped toward her, then stopped exactly where he had promised himself he would stop—close enough to be there, not so close as to pretend the boundary didn’t matter.

“You’re not a mistake,” he said.

Her expression changed.

Just for a second.

The sarcasm almost returned, but not quite.

“That was dangerously sincere,” she said.

“Yeah.”

“You should apologize.”

“No.”

“Rude.”

“Publicly visible,” Touma said, with great bitterness. “You’ve already ruined my image. We’re past manners.”

Mio stared at him.

Then, despite everything, she laughed.

It was small.

It was shaky.

It sounded like fear trying to pass itself off as mockery because it had nowhere else to go.

Touma felt the laugh land in his chest like something warm and dangerous.

For one very brief second, the room became bearable again.

Then the hallway outside the room made a soft scraping sound.

Not footsteps.

Not a door.

Something gentler than that.

Like a fingertip dragging along the other side of a wall.

Touma froze.

Mio’s laughter died instantly.

Her head snapped toward the sound.

The wall beside the bedroom door seemed, impossibly, to take on a faint pressure in the plaster.

The safehouse lights flickered once.

Touma felt the old familiar cold coin of the limiter behind his skull pulse in a way that had never felt so much like warning.

Mio’s voice dropped to a whisper.

“Touma,” she said, suddenly and unmistakably afraid, “don’t let the system decide what I am.”

And then the wall answered.

Not with words.

With a second, deeper scrape from inside the plaster, as if something on the other side had heard her and was leaning closer.

### 06 — The Safety Interview

Touma had expected the worst part of the day to be the part where Kureha Sanjo arrived.

That was a respectable expectation. It had a spine.
It had logic.
It was the sort of expectation that should have been rewarded by the universe out of basic decency.

Instead the worst part turned out to be the ten minutes before Kureha arrived, when the safehouse was still pretending to be a safehouse and not the kind of place where a bad decision could sit in a chair and ask for tea.

Touma stood in the kitchen area with a paper cup of instant coffee he was not drinking, because drinking would mean admitting he could taste things again, and Mio was hovering near the table with her arms folded so hard she looked like she was trying to keep herself from falling apart by force of attitude alone.
His free hand kept checking for the cheap navy-blue star in his pocket and abandoning it again the instant he felt the acrylic edge, like even comfort had become evidence.

Nico had turned the main room into a temporary interview station.

He had a folding table, two extra chairs, a slim recorder, a diagnostic slate, and the expression of a man who had just discovered that every tool in his professional life could be reclassified as a weapon if the wrong person smiled at it.

Touma looked around the room and said, “This is not reassuring.”

“It is not meant to be reassuring,” Nico said.

“Then why did you arrange it like a conference room that hates me?”

“Because that is what Kureha will recognize.”

Mio gave a tiny, unpleasant hum. “So we are decorating for the predator.”

Nico pointed at her without looking up from the recorder. “That did not sound surprised.”

Touma took a sip of coffee anyway. It was terrible, which at least gave the world some consistency.

The problem, of course, was that Kureha had not yet done anything overtly hostile.

He had sent a message.

A polite one.

That was the issue.

`Requesting a brief safety interview regarding post-procedural cognition stabilization. No disruption intended.`

Touma had read it twice, then a third time, then once more with the sort of disgust normally reserved for bugs in sealed food.

Mio had read it once and immediately said, “That is a threat.”

Nico had rubbed his face with both hands and muttered, “Yes.”

Touma had asked, “Then why are we letting him come here?”

And Nico, which was becoming his favorite way to be infuriated, had answered, “Because if we refuse, he will do this somewhere else with less warning and better paperwork.”

So now they were waiting.

Touma hated waiting.

Waiting gave his brain room to imagine the shape of whatever came next, and his brain had not demonstrated good judgment lately.

Mio was watching the doorway with the kind of stillness that meant she was trying very hard not to look like she was watching the doorway.

Touma leaned a shoulder against the kitchenette counter and said quietly, “You don’t have to stand there like that.”

Mio didn’t look at him. “Like what?”

“Like you’re guarding the room.”

“I am guarding the room.”

“From what?”

She finally turned her head. Her expression was dry, but the tension under it had not gone away since she became visible.

“From the man who uses ‘safety’ the way a butcher uses a clean apron.”

Touma made a face. “That’s a terrible image.”

“I know. It is also accurate.”

He wanted to argue.
He didn’t.

The door chimed.

Nico straightened so fast his chair skidded an inch.

Touma’s stomach dropped before the lock even disengaged.

The door opened.

Kureha Sanjo stepped in carrying a slim document case and the expression of someone arriving to inspect a room he had already decided belonged to him.

He looked tidy in the way dangerous people often did when they wanted to seem harmless.
Pale coat.
Neat hair.
Gloves.
The sort of calm face that suggested he had never once raised his voice because he had never needed to.

His eyes moved over the room once.
The table.
The recorder.
Touma.
Nico.
Then they landed on Mio.

There was a fractional pause.
Not surprise.
Recognition.

Touma felt his skin go cold.

Kureha smiled.

It was not a friendly smile.
It was a person making a note that something had unexpectedly become usable.

“Good,” Kureha said. “So the embodiment took.”

Touma’s hand twitched hard enough to spill coffee over his knuckles.
He didn’t even notice until Mio said, sharply, “Touma.”

He looked down, swore, and set the cup aside.

Nico moved first. “Sanjo. Sit down.”

Kureha’s gaze flicked to him. “You invited me into a room with an uncontained projection and told me to sit down.”

“That is the current arrangement.”

Kureha’s smile deepened by a millimeter. “I see the improvisation has not improved since last time.”

Nico did not answer that.
That, Touma thought, was probably wise.

Kureha closed the door behind himself with measured care and set the document case on the table as if he were arriving to conduct a clinic review rather than a possible kidnapping.

Touma hated him on sight.
Not because Kureha was loud.
He wasn’t.
Not because he looked monstrous.
He didn’t.
He was worse than that.
He looked like a man who believed his methods had already been forgiven by the future.

Kureha took in Touma’s posture, then Mio’s, then the space between them.

“Interesting,” he said again.

Mio’s mouth flattened.
“Say that one more time and I will make this interview very educational.”

“Threatening language from a newly externalized cognitive interface,” Kureha said pleasantly. “That is a good sign.”

Touma stared at him. “How is that a good sign?”

“It means she has retained personality continuity under stress.”

Mio’s eyes narrowed to slits.
Touma could feel her anger like static in the room.

Kureha continued as if he had not noticed. “If the projection had collapsed into compliance scripting, we would have a problem.”

Mio turned her head a fraction toward him. Her holographic outline shimmered in irritation. “You don’t say.”

Touma snapped his gaze back to her. “You can talk to him?”

“Apparently I can talk to anyone in the room,” Mio said, and then, after a beat, “I hate this.”

That was so close to panic that Touma’s chest squeezed painfully.

She looked around the lab once, too fast, like she was measuring where the edges were and finding them all wrong. The room had gone suddenly crowded with surfaces it had never bothered to admit she could notice: cabinet handles, cable runs, the angle of the ceiling lights reflected in the glass, Nico’s stylus sitting at the edge of the console like a tiny accusation.

That was the first thing Touma realized about her body made visible.

She noticed everything.

The second thing was worse: she was trying not to.

“Can you touch anything?” he asked.

Mio looked at him like he had asked a question with an obvious wrong answer.

“I don’t know.”

That was worse than no.

Touma reached toward her before he had finished deciding to do it.

His hand passed through her shoulder.

The air sparked cold across his fingers, like brushing through static off a winter blanket.

Mio flinched.

Touma jerked back. “Sorry.”

“Don’t apologize,” Mio said immediately, then seemed to regret the speed of it. “It was informative.”

“Informative,” Touma repeated, because if he did not repeat things his head might go somewhere lethal.

Nico finally moved. He took one step away from the console, one slow breath in, and said, “The interface decoupled into a projected cognitive shell.”

Touma pointed at Mio. “In plain language.”

“In plain language,” Nico said, sounding faintly sick, “the limiter didn’t delete her. It separated her into a readable externalized state.”

Mio’s mouth tightened.

Touma’s pulse climbed.

“Readable,” he said. “You make her sound like a file.”

Nico did not take the bait. “At the moment, she is partially legible to the system. That is not the same thing as stable.”

Mio’s head turned sharply.

Her eyes—bright, pale, a little too reflective—fixed on Nico.

“Say that again,” she said.

Nico looked at her. Really looked. The man’s face had gone very still, but there was no spectacle in it. Just the steady horror of someone watching a boundary he had expected to hold fail in real time.

“Your current embodiment is tied to Touma’s limiter architecture,” he said. “If the system destabilizes further, you could collapse with it.”

Touma’s stomach dropped so hard it felt like a physical injury.

Mio’s expression did not change.

That was the frightening part.

She had gone too quiet.

Touma knew that quiet. He knew it in the way he knew a storm front by the pressure in his molars.

But now the quiet looked different on her face. It looked like she was counting how many ways the room could take her back.

“Mio,” he said.

She didn’t look at him.

Nico exhaled once, short. “We need to bring the system back within bounds before it starts interpreting the projection as a revision target.”

Touma blinked. “A what?”

Nico’s eyes flicked to the monitor.

“An authority problem,” he said.

Mio gave a humorless little laugh. “That is such an ugly phrase.”

Touma’s head snapped around. “Why are you sounding amused?”

“Because if I panic,” she said, very carefully, “I will make it worse.”

“That sounds like a personal threat to my peace of mind.”

“It is.”

He should have been relieved she was still herself enough to be rude.
Instead, that made the fear sharper.

Touma stepped off the chair platform. The restraint leads snapped free with a soft click. He nearly tripped over his own feet trying to get closer to her.

Mio’s eyes tracked him. She looked less like a machine now and more like a person standing in a room that had never expected to admit she existed.

He had the sudden absurd thought that if he touched her long enough, she might feel warm.

That thought hit like a shove.

He stopped a hand’s breadth away.

“Are you real?” he asked before he could stop himself.

Mio looked at him for a long second.

Then, with a sharpness that was almost anger, she said, “You are asking the wrong question.”

He frowned. “What’s the right one?”

Whether he could hear it.

But she didn’t say that.
Instead she said, “Whether I still function.”

Touma hated the answer because it sounded too much like her.

Nico cleared his throat from the other side of the room. “We need to test interaction stability. Touma, do not use Unthink unless absolutely necessary.”

Touma stared at him. “Why would I use Unthink right now?”

“Because your stress response is already rising.”

“I can control myself.”

Mio and Nico said, in the same flat tone and at the exact same moment, “No, you cannot.”

Touma pointed at both of them. “That is wildly unfair.”

“It is also correct,” Mio said.

He glared at her. “You are enjoying being a person now.”

“I am enjoying being right in three dimensions.”

There it was.
The smallest possible return of her usual tone.
Touma could have cried with relief, which was inconvenient and humiliating and probably true.

Nico moved closer, slowly, like he was approaching a live wire and trying not to insult it.
He held out a diagnostic strip toward Mio.

She looked at it, then at him.

“You want me to cooperate,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Do you have a phrase for asking consent that does not sound like procurement?”

Nico shut his eyes for one beat.
Touma choked on a laugh he did not mean to have.

Nico opened his eyes again. “May I?”

Mio stared at him.

Touma felt, unexpectedly, a flare of pride on her behalf that was immediately followed by embarrassment at having pride on her behalf in front of her.

After a pause, she held out her hand.

Nico froze.

Touma, stupidly, said, “Take it before she changes her mind.”

Mio shot him a look. “That was not supportive.”

“It was practical.”

“It was rude.”

“It was both.”

Nico did not touch her skin at first. The strip hovered against the edge of her hand, and the machine chirped softly.

Mio watched the contact point like she was bracing for a verdict. The old joke-face was still there, but underneath it something had gone rigid. Touma could see the effort in the set of her shoulders, in the way she kept her fingers from curling inward, in the fact that she had not yet asked whether this hurt.

That was the other thing her embodiment made obvious.
She was trying to behave like she had already decided she was not allowed to need anything.

“Interesting,” Nico murmured.

Mio’s smile sharpened. “That is the sort of word people use right before they ruin a life.”

Touma snorted despite himself.

Nico ignored that and rotated the strip a few degrees, like he was reading a patient’s pulse rather than the edge of a person’s right to remain herself. “The projection has tactile responsiveness. Low-level sensation matrix is intact. That is useful.”

“Useful,” Mio echoed. “I feel cherished.”

“Don’t start,” Touma muttered.

She glanced at him. “I wasn’t starting. I was surviving.”

He hated how quickly that landed.

Then the monitor behind Nico threw a warning.

Every line on the screen jerked hard at once.

Mio’s form flickered.

Touma’s stomach lurched. “What was that?”

Nico snapped his head up. “The limiter is still actively referencing her as a stabilization node.”

Touma’s breath caught. “Can it do that?”

“It is doing it.” Nico’s voice had gone tight. “It’s trying to reconcile the projection with the base function.”

Mio’s body stuttered at the edges like a badly rendered image.

She looked down at her own hand.

For a second, the fingers blurred into light.

Touma moved before he thought better of it and grabbed her wrist.

A shock went through him, not electricity exactly, but the sickening sensation of his own thoughts trying to slip sideways. The room dimmed at the edges. A name tried to rise in him and failed to catch.

He clenched hard enough to hurt.

Mio snapped her gaze to him.

“Touma—”

“I know.”

He didn’t, not really.

What he knew was that the moment he let go, she might go with the flicker.
What he knew was that the panic trying to rise in her face was doing something mean to his heart.
What he knew was that Kureha was watching all of it like a man standing at the edge of a lab notebook, waiting for the page to turn useful.

Mio’s voice dropped. “If the system keeps pulling on me, I will lose coherence.”

“Can you hold?” Touma asked.

Her mouth twisted.
“Very flattering confidence. No, Touma. I can endure, but I cannot endure forever.”

That landed harder than if she had screamed.

Because she sounded careful.
Because she sounded like she had already learned the shape of her own damage.

The monitor chirped again, higher this time.

Nico swore under his breath. “Kureha is still feeding the threshold in the background.”

Touma looked up. “What does that mean?”

“It means he has been adjusting the environmental instability to provoke a response without making it look like an attack.”

Mio’s eyes narrowed. “So the interview was bait.”

Kureha, who had been quiet enough to become nearly invisible, folded his hands and said, “It was also an opportunity.”

Touma turned on him so fast the room seemed to snap into place around it. “You did this on purpose?”

Kureha’s tone stayed mild. “I created conditions under which your system would reveal what it can survive.”

Mio’s face changed.
Just a little.
Enough.
Fear, then anger, then something like disgust.
Touma had never seen all three at once on her before.

“You set me up,” she said.

Kureha inclined his head. “I tested you.”

Touma’s grip tightened around her wrist until he realized he was hurting her and loosened by force.
The instant he did, her outline frayed harder.
Mio made a sharp, involuntary sound and grabbed his sleeve with her free hand.

That tiny movement did more to him than Kureha’s smugness ever could.

She needed him steady.
That was awful.
That was intimate.
That was not a role either of them had consented to discover like this.

Touma swallowed.
His mouth had gone dry enough to taste blood.

He looked at the monitor, then at Nico, then at Mio’s shaking outline.
And somewhere under the panic, under the fear of losing her and the uglier fear of what Kureha had just proven, something smaller and colder clicked into place.

This was not just a projection problem.
This was not just a rescue problem.
This was a coherence problem.

A person problem.

Mio’s fingers dug into his sleeve hard enough to make the fabric wrinkle.
“Touma,” she said, and for once there was no joke in it. “If you have a way to make this stop, do it now.”

He looked at her face.
Really looked.
The whole room seemed to pull back, leaving only the brightness of her eyes and the fact that those eyes could now be harmed.

He thought of every time he had let her be the thing that paid first.
He thought of every time he had told himself he could handle it later.
He thought of the hideous possibility that if he used his power wrong here, he might save the shape of the room and lose the reason he had wanted to save it at all.

Touma took one breath.
Then another.

And in that narrow, shaking margin between panic and choice, he finally understood what the room had been telling him since Mio first appeared in it:

he could not stabilize her by pretending she was only a system,
and he could not protect her by treating her like something already gone.

He met her eyes.
“I’m going to try something,” he said.

Mio’s grip tightened once. “Try carefully.”

That almost made him smile.
Almost.
Instead he looked at the pressure line climbing on the monitor, centered his thoughts the way Nico had drilled into him, and reached for the exact shape of the instability he needed to cut.

Not her.
Not the room.
Not the fear.

The failure that was making her blur.

The moment his focus locked, the first edge of Unthink stirred in him like a blade being drawn from a sleeve.

Then Kureha smiled.

Not broadly.
Not theatrically.
Just enough for Touma to realize, all at once, that the man had been waiting for this exact shape of desperation.

“There,” Kureha said softly. “That reaction.”

Touma dragged his gaze off Mio long enough to look at him.
“What did you do?”

Kureha folded his hands as if Touma had finally asked a sensible question.
“I adjusted the stress profile. Barely. The threshold system does the rest on its own once it recognizes the right kind of contradiction.”

Nico went white.

Mio’s expression sharpened with sudden, ugly understanding.

“You’re spoofing the limiter,” she said.

Kureha inclined his head.
“Not crudely. That would be vulgar. I am only speaking in a dialect it already trusts.”

Touma stared at him. “You’re telling me you can make her override rules fire because you know how the implant listens?”

“I am telling you,” Kureha said, still pleasant, “that your stabilization architecture is not a mystery to everyone in the room.”

Nico’s hand tightened around the edge of the console hard enough for his knuckles to pale. “You kept calibration access.”

Kureha glanced at him.
“I kept useful access.”

Touma felt something in the room rearrange itself around that sentence.

This wasn’t just a threatening little visit.
It wasn’t even just a test.
It was proof.
Kureha could reach inside the rule that decided when Mio was allowed to act and bend it until her protection looked like betrayal, or his attack looked like a medical event.

Mio went very still.
That stillness scared Touma more than the flicker in her outline had.

Because now he could see the exact moment she understood what it meant.
Not only for this room.
For every room after this one.

Kureha’s gaze moved between them with methodical interest.
“You are both treating this like a crisis,” he said. “It is more accurate to call it clarification.”

Touma laughed once, sharp and joyless. “You should be illegal in several religions.”

“Possibly.”

Mio’s voice cut in before Touma could move.
“Don’t,” she said.

He looked at her.
She was looking at Kureha, not him, and that was somehow worse.

“If you lunge at him now,” she said, “he gets exactly what he wants. A spike. An excuse. A cleaner read.”

Touma’s jaw clenched.
“So what, I stand here?”

“For ten more seconds,” Mio said. “Yes.”

That tone did it.
Not because it was commanding.
Because it wasn’t.
Because it sounded like someone balancing terror on a knife-edge and refusing to drop it in front of him.

Touma made himself stay still.
It felt like swallowing broken glass.

Kureha noticed.
Of course he noticed.

“There,” he said again, almost to himself. “Mutual regulation under pressure. More advanced than the reports suggested.”

Touma’s hands curled into fists.
“You don’t get to narrate us.”

Kureha looked at him as if Touma had just volunteered an additional useful symptom.
“That remains to be seen.”

The monitor behind Nico gave a thin warning chirp.
Not a collapse.
Not yet.
But enough to prove the room had not gone back to normal.

Nico looked at the screen, then at Touma, then at Mio.
When he spoke, his voice was controlled so tightly it was almost bloodless.

“He’s right about one thing,” Nico said. “This is clarification.”

Touma turned on him. “Whose side are you on?”

“Yours,” Nico snapped. “Which is why I am telling you the ugly version now instead of the comforting one later.” He pointed at the monitor. “He’s shown he can perturb the threshold logic without tripping obvious attack signatures. That means every future override event is compromised until we prove otherwise.”

Touma felt the sentence hit his bones.

Every future override event.

Every time Mio stopped him.
Every time she protected him.
Every time she crossed the line because she thought he was about to break.

Kureha could poison the meaning of all of it.

Mio said nothing.
That was how Touma knew the same thought had already hit her harder.

Kureha lifted his document case and rested it lightly on the table.
“I did not come here merely to threaten you,” he said. “I came because your current condition is unstable, novel, and ethically too important to leave to amateurs.”

Touma stared. “I think I actually hate the word ‘ethically’ now.”

“That’s healthy,” Mio said faintly.

It was such a small, mean little line.
So recognizably her.
Touma almost broke from the relief of it.

Kureha opened the case.
Inside were slim diagnostic wafers, a pen-sized projector, a thin yellow-banded Union maintenance key with its service chevrons sanded half-off, and a palm-sized instrument dark as smoked glass.
Nico saw it and inhaled like he had been struck.

“That credential was supposed to be burned,” Nico said.

Kureha’s eyes did not leave Touma and Mio.
“Oh, yes.”

Touma pointed at the instrument. “What is that?”

Nico answered before Kureha could.
“Calibration hardware.”

The room went colder.

Not physically.
Structurally.
Like the shape of the danger had finally chosen a name.

Kureha set two fingers on the smoked glass device with the casual familiarity of a man touching his own keys.
“Now,” he said, “we can begin the real safety interview.”

### 07 — Understood

The interview began the way a mugging might begin if the knife apologized first.

Kureha Sanjo sat at Nico’s folding table with the calibration device resting beside one gloved hand and spoke in the low, measured tone of a man who considered panic an inefficiency other people indulged in for his benefit.

Touma hated him immediately for making the room quieter just by deciding it should be.

Nico remained standing near the monitor, rigid enough to look bolted there. Mio had taken up a position half a step behind Touma’s shoulder, which would have looked casual to anyone who had never watched a person try to turn dread into posture.

Kureha clicked on the recorder.

“State your current baseline,” he said.

Touma stared at him. “No.”

Kureha made a small note on the slate in front of him. “Defensive presentation intact.”

“I am going to hit you with that recorder.”

“Threat response proportionate. Useful.”

Touma’s hands twitched.

Mio said quietly, “Do not satisfy him so quickly. Make him work for it.”

Kureha looked up at that.
Not startled. Interested.

“Yes,” he said. “That’s the more impressive part of the architecture.”

Mio’s mouth flattened. “I am not an architecture.”

“Not exclusively, no.”

Nico stepped in before Touma could throw something. “You said you wanted a safety interview. Ask an actual safety question.”

Kureha’s gaze drifted to him. “Very well.” He looked back at Touma. “When did the projection first exhibit externally stable self-report?”

Touma blinked. “That isn’t a safety question.”

“It is if I’m trying to determine whether she is a transient stabilization effect or a persistent interpretive agent.”

“She,” Touma said, with deliberate emphasis.

Kureha smiled without warmth. “Touching.”

Touma felt the room pull tight around his ribs.

Mio answered before he could.
“Immediately after embodiment,” she said. “And before you ask, yes, my continuity remained intact. No, I am not going to let you inspect it like produce.”

Kureha made another note.
“Humor, antagonism, self-reference, loyalty orientation.”

Touma took one step forward.
Nico put a hand on his arm before he got farther.

“Don’t,” Nico muttered.

Touma kept his eyes on Kureha. “You make one more note about her like that and I’m going to invent a new category of assault.”

“That reaction,” Kureha said, “is exactly why this is worth doing.”

Mio gave a short, disbelieving laugh. “He says ‘worth’ the way other people say ‘profitable.’”

Kureha turned the smoked-glass calibrator over once in his hand.

Touma noticed it because Mio noticed it.
He felt the shift in her body before he consciously understood why: not movement, exactly, but a sudden concentration, like all of her attention had narrowed to the object in Kureha’s hand and the ugly history implied by how comfortable he looked holding it.

“Put that down,” Nico said.

Kureha did not.

“I have no intention of using it crudely,” he said.

“That is not reassuring language,” Touma said.

“It is honest language.”

Touma hated that honesty could be used to make a room dirtier.

Kureha set the device flat on the table.
Nothing visible happened.

Touma nevertheless felt something tiny go wrong.
Not pain.
Not sound.
A misalignment. Like the room had tilted a few invisible degrees and only his nervous system had noticed.

Mio inhaled sharply.

Touma turned toward her. “What?”

She did not answer immediately.
That alone made Nico move.

“What did you do?” Nico demanded.

“Very little,” Kureha said. “I’m only introducing a diagnostic tension to see which layer compensates first.”

Touma looked from the table to Mio to Nico and hated that those words almost sounded clinical enough to hide inside.

Then Mio grabbed the back of Touma’s sleeve hard enough to wrinkle the fabric.
At some point in the last hour, contact had stopped being impossible and become unreliable instead.

That was not subtle.
Mio did not do non-subtle unless something had gone badly wrong.

“Touma,” she said.

He looked at her and saw it at once.
Her outline had not begun to flicker the way it had before.
This was worse.
She was too sharp.
Too over-resolved, like the world had decided to render her with cruel attention.
Every edge of her face was precise enough to cut.

“What is it?” he asked.

“He’s tuning the room toward contradiction.”

Kureha’s mouth twitched. “Good.”

Touma rounded on him. “Stop saying good.”

Nico was already at the console. His hands moved fast over the controls, trying to isolate whatever the calibrator was feeding into the safehouse system.

“It isn’t a direct signal,” he said. “He’s piggybacking on an old Union maintenance handshake. The calibrator is using service-layer privileges to perturb the interpretive threshold.”

Touma stared.

“In English,” Nico said, not looking up, “the architecture is starting to argue with itself about what counts as stable because the room thinks the order came from inside the house.”

Touma stared. “In English.”

Mio answered this time.
“In English, the room is being taught to become suspicious.”

That was such a terrible way to phrase it that Touma understood immediately.

Suspicious of what belonged.
Suspicious of what was real.
Suspicious of what should be allowed to persist.

Kureha folded his hands again. “You can, of course, stop this at any time.”

Touma glared. “How generous.”

“Cooperate,” Kureha said. “Answer cleanly. Let the system settle around truth instead of resistance.”

Mio’s laugh this time held actual contempt. “He means submit in a way that flatters his vocabulary.”

Kureha ignored her.
“Question one. Touma Kamijou: when you experience onset instability, what is the first symptom you reliably notice?”

Touma held his stare because it was safer than answering.

Kureha waited.

The room leaned harder.
Not physically. Cognitively.
Touma suddenly had the bizarre, nauseating sensation that the cup on the counter behind him had become more conceptually important than his own hands. The floor light seemed too loud. Nico’s breathing from across the room sounded like a piece of equipment trying to impersonate a person.

Mio’s grip tightened again.

Touma swallowed.
“Headache,” he said.

Kureha nodded. “Good. Secondary?”

“Noise.”

“External or internal?”

“Yes,” Touma snapped.

Kureha wrote that down like it was a clever answer instead of a hostile one.

“Projection response,” he said.

Mio’s voice went colder. “Stop calling me that.”

“Then supply a better operational term.”

“Person,” Touma said.

“Loaded term.”

Touma took another step.
Nico caught him again.

“Don’t,” Nico hissed. “Not while he’s tuning it.”

Touma jerked his arm away anyway, but he stopped moving.
Not because Nico was right.
Because Mio was looking at him with that expression again: the one that meant she was terrified and budgeting her terror so he could keep functioning.

Kureha watched the exchange with naked concentration now.
Not detached curiosity.
Pattern hunger.

“Interesting,” he murmured.

Mio said, “If you say that again, I am going to redefine your kneecaps.”

And then the room broke.

Not explosively.
Not dramatically.
A small sound came out of the console—just a soft wrong click—and Nico went abruptly still.

Touma noticed it because silence around Nico was never a good sign.

“Nico?” he said.

No response.

Nico was staring at the main monitor with the face of a man who had just been handed a sentence he could read but not parse.
His pupils were wide. His breathing had gone shallow. One hand remained on the keyboard, unmoving, as if he had forgotten how hands worked halfway through using it.

Touma felt the room shift again.
Suddenly every object had too many possible meanings. The console wasn’t a console, it was a surface where decisions became consequences. The recorder wasn’t a recorder, it was proof with a pulse. Kureha’s gloves weren’t gloves, they were sterile permission.

He hated it.
He hated that the world was becoming metaphor at him.

“Mio,” he said.

But she was already moving.

Not fast.
That was the strange part.
She stepped past him with terrifying clarity, every line of her body intent without being rushed. She looked at Nico once, then at the monitor, then at the calibrator in Kureha’s hand.

Touma expected anger.
What she wore instead was focus.
A colder thing. Cleaner.

Kureha noticed too.
His voice went almost reverent.
“There it is.”

Mio ignored him.

“Nico,” she said.

No answer.

She took one more step toward him.
Touma could feel the room resisting it, as if every surface wanted to force her back into the category of anomaly instead of letting her behave like a person crossing a room to help someone.

Then Mio spoke again.

Not louder.
Not in a different language.
Just with an authority Touma had never heard from her before.

“Nico Arata,” she said, “look at me.”

He did.
Slowly. Like surfacing through black water.

Mio kept her eyes on him.
The sharpness in the room bent around her voice.

“You are in the safehouse,” she said. “Kureha is manipulating the threshold. The monitor is lying by exaggeration. Your hands still work. Your name is still yours. Breathe once and keep the room small.”

Touma felt it happen.

Not a shock.
Not a blast.
A settling.

The impossible surplus of meaning draining out of the furniture.
The room becoming a room again instead of a conspiracy of symbols.
The light returning to its proper brightness.
The air choosing to be air.

Nico inhaled.
A real inhale.
He blinked hard, looked at the monitor, and recoiled like a man seeing a hallucination after being told it had edges.

“What,” he said hoarsely, “did you just do?”

Mio answered without looking away from him.
“I made the distortion stop pretending to be inevitable.”

Kureha stood up.
Not abruptly. That would have been human.
But he stood.

Touma turned toward him and saw something he had not expected to see on Kureha Sanjo’s face.
Not kindness.
Not fear.

Delight.

That frightened Touma more than if the man had pulled a weapon.

Kureha spoke softly, like he was afraid volume might damage the evidence.
“Not limiter behavior,” he said. “Not override. Coherence restoration.”

Mio finally looked at him.
“What did you think would happen?”

Kureha’s smile came back, but thinner now. Truer.
“I thought you might have become a constraint engine with personality residue.”

Touma took a step toward him. “And?”

“And,” Kureha said, not taking his eyes off Mio, “I was wrong.”

That should not have sounded like danger.
It did.

Nico had recovered enough to slap a manual block over part of the console feed. The smoked-glass calibrator buzzed angrily in Kureha’s hand.

“Shut it down,” Nico said.

Kureha did not move.

Touma was already there before he realized he had crossed the room.
He put himself between Kureha and Mio with enough force to make the chair legs scrape.

“Interview over,” Touma said.

Kureha considered him.
Then, finally, switched off the device.

The room eased another fraction.
Not all the way.
But enough that Touma could feel his teeth unclench.

Mio swayed.

Touma turned so fast he almost collided with her.
“Hey.”

She made an irritated sound. “Don’t say hey like I am a staircase accident.”

“That was not a no.”

“It was a stylistic objection.”

But her voice had gone thinner. Not weak. Stretched.
As if whatever she had just done had required her to hold more of the room together than she had wanted him to notice.

Her outline gave a small, ugly shimmer at the edges.
Not enough to lose her.
Enough to make Touma understand, with a cold little drop in his stomach, that keeping Nico intact had cost her something measurable.

He reached for her on instinct and stopped half an inch short, suddenly unsure whether touch would steady her or make the strain worse.
That hesitation felt obscene.
He hated not knowing the rules of her body.
He hated even more that Kureha was watching them learn those rules in real time.

Mio noticed the halted hand.
For one second, the expression that crossed her face was too unguarded to survive daylight.
Not embarrassment.
Not pain alone.
The much worse thing underneath both: the effort of staying legible while visibly paying for it.

Then she pulled herself narrower, sharper.
Not recovered.
Contained.

Nico saw it too.
“You stabilized me,” he said.

Mio glanced at him. “Unfortunately.”

“No,” Nico said, and for once there was no clinical distance in him at all. “I mean it literally. I know what the threshold distortions feel like. That wasn’t suppression. You gave the system back enough coherence to sort signal from panic.”

Touma looked between them. “So what does that mean?”

No one answered for a second.

Then Kureha did.

“It means,” he said, gathering his recorder with maddening calm, “that she is not merely limiting damage. She is resolving it.”

Mio’s face hardened.
Nico’s had gone pale again.
Touma felt the phrase land before he understood it.

Resolving it.

Mio said the word first.
Not loudly.
Not like a declaration.
Like someone touching the outline of a truth and finding it already warm.

“Understood,” she said.

The room went silent.

Touma looked at her.
The word fit too quickly.
Too cleanly.
As if it had been waiting in the walls for the right mouth to say it.

Kureha’s attention sharpened to a needlepoint.
“Yes,” he said. “That is much closer.”

Touma rounded on him. “Get out.”

Kureha slipped the recorder back into his case.
“I will,” he said. “For now.”

That phrase made Touma want violence on a spiritual level.

At the door, Kureha paused and looked back at Mio.

“Do be careful,” he said. “Powers that clarify often discover, too late, what they themselves depend on.”

Then he left.

The latch clicked shut.

For three full seconds no one moved.

Then Touma said, with enormous sincerity, “I want to break his face into educational shapes.”

Nico sat down so suddenly the folding chair protested. “Take a number.”

Mio did not laugh.

Touma looked at her and the absence of the laugh hit him harder than anything Kureha had said.

“Mio.”

She straightened by force. “I am fine.”

“That sounded fake.”

“It was meant to.”

Nico dragged one hand over his face. “We need to write down exactly what just happened before the room starts lying about it by omission.”

Touma ignored him.
“Did that hurt?” he asked.

Mio looked at him for a long moment.
Then, because she apparently still believed truth was a weapon best used precisely, she said, “Yes.”

Touma’s chest tightened.

“But,” she added, before he could say anything unwise, “not in the way you think.”

That did not help.

Nico reached for the recorder again, then stopped and deliberately pushed it away.
“That wasn’t override logic,” he said quietly. “You didn’t force Nico back into compliance. You reduced distortion until he could become himself again.”

Mio said nothing.

Touma watched her instead.
The way her outline had gone steady again.
The way her shoulders were still too rigid.
The way she was looking not at either of them, but at her own hands.

As if she had just discovered they were capable of carrying something dangerous.

Later, when Nico was in the back room swearing at logs and pretending not to give them privacy, Touma found Mio standing near the safehouse window.
The glass held a faint reflection of her projection: pale face, bright eyes, edges of light where a body should have had ordinary shadow.

For a moment he just stood there with her.

“You were terrifying,” he said finally.

Mio’s mouth twitched. “That is not usually your compliment register.”

“I mean it nicely.”

“That seems structurally unstable.”

He let out a breath that could almost have been a laugh.
Then he said, quieter, “You saved him.”

Mio kept looking at the window.
“I stabilized him.”

“That is a synonym in this room.”

She was silent for a beat too long.

Then she said, “No. It isn’t.”

Touma turned toward her.
The sharpness had returned to her face, but private this time. Not aimed outward. Held inward.

“When I did it,” she said, “I could feel what the room was trying to make of him. Noise. panic. contradiction. And I knew how to narrow it until it became something he could survive.”

Touma waited.

Mio looked down at her own hand.
For an instant her fingertips lost definition, then resolved again.

Very slight.
Easy to miss.
He did not miss it.

“And?” he asked.

Mio answered so softly he almost thought the glass had said it for her.

“And the same logic is what is keeping me here.”

Touma felt the floor go uncertain under him.

She finally looked at him then.
No joke. No edge. No decorative cruelty.
Just the truth, standing in front of him without armor.

“If I stop being able to hold coherence,” Mio said, “I don’t think I disappear all at once.”

His throat tightened.

“How, then?”

Mio’s expression went strangely gentle.
Which scared him more than if she had flinched.

“I think,” she said, “I become less understandable until the room wins.”

Touma stared at her.
Then at her hand.
Then back at her face.

Understood.

The most beautiful and horrifying thing he had heard all week.

He took one step closer.
Not enough to touch.
Enough to make the distance a decision instead of an accident.

“Then we don’t let the room win,” he said.

Mio looked at him for a long time.

Then, with a fragility she would absolutely deny under torture, she said, “Try to say that like you mean it less.”

Touma did not.

### 08 — Two Halves of a Worse Miracle

Nico insisted on rules.

Touma hated rules most when they were reasonable.

“No live public network,” Nico said, pacing the safehouse workroom with a tablet in one hand and the harried expression of a man trying to build a seatbelt after already hitting the wall. “No active threshold perturbation. No direct emotional trigger material. No improvising once the test begins. If anything drifts, we stop.”

Touma leaned back in his chair and folded his arms. “You say ‘we’ as if I’m the one who keeps bringing cursed hardware into the apartment.”

“I say ‘we’ because if this goes badly, it will not respect pronouns.”

Fair.
Annoying, but fair.

Mio sat on the edge of the desk with one knee drawn up slightly, watching Nico pace like he was a documentary about professional regret.
Since Kureha’s visit she had been steadier in some ways and more dangerous in others. Not externally. Internally. The room seemed to stop getting the first move around her now. She had become less easy to blur.

That should have been comforting.
Instead Touma kept noticing how much effort the steadiness cost.

Nico stopped pacing. “We know two things.”

Touma raised a hand. “I object already.”

“I know.”

Nico tapped the tablet.
“First: Kureha can spoof or perturb threshold conditions with calibration hardware from the same family as the original tuning apparatus. Second: Mio’s coherence-restoration effect is real. It isn’t just override logic. It narrows distortion.”

Mio said, “That was the optimistic phrasing.”

Nico nodded once. “The less optimistic phrasing is that we now know you can stabilize the same kind of interpretive failure that is also holding you together.”

Touma looked at Mio.
She didn’t look back.

There it was.
The sentence none of them liked touching directly.

Nico continued before the room could sink under it.
“We need to know whether your effects can be used together intentionally instead of only during panic. If Touma can cut a distortion source and Mio can clarify target boundaries at the same time, we may be able to neutralize future threshold attacks without waiting for a near-collapse.”

Touma frowned. “May.”

“Yes.”

“That’s a bad word.”

“It’s an honest one.”

Touma muttered, “I’m surrounded by people who weaponize honesty.”

Mio glanced at him. “As if you don’t contribute.”

“Not with this much posture.”

Nico exhaled through his nose and set the tablet down on the workbench. “I am proposing a bounded test. Small target. No live subjects. No memory-loaded material. We induce a controlled interpretive contradiction in the room, Touma cuts only the false persistence, and Mio keeps the effect narrow.”

Touma stared.
“You found a way to make my life sound like a lab rat with trust issues.”

“That is because your life is, at present, a lab rat with trust issues.”

Mio gave a tiny approving hum. “That was good, Nico. Don’t ruin it by becoming likable.”

Touma looked from one of them to the other and felt a brief, irritating surge of something almost like relief.

They sounded normal.
Not safe.
Not fine.
But normal enough to insult each other in familiar shapes.

He hated how much that mattered.

For three whole seconds nobody said anything.
The old fridge in the kitchenette kicked once and settled, and Touma hated how grateful he felt for one ordinary sound in a room that had been refusing to behave like a room.

“So what’s the target?” he asked.

Nico reached into the case on the bench and took out a slim Union tag: white polymer, serial strip along one edge, inert until paired to a live system.

“I’ve loaded it with a synthetic contradiction marker,” Nico said. “The room should read it as two status states at once. Nothing human. Nothing relational. Just enough interpretive noise to test whether you can sever the false overlap without damaging the carrier.”

Touma made a face. “You found the least romantic version of all of this.”

“That,” Mio said, “is why he survived long enough to become useful.”

Nico set the tag on the center of the table inside a thin chalk-white square he’d marked with sensor tape.

Touma eyed it.
“That tiny thing is going to ruin my afternoon?”

“If it works,” Nico said, “it may ruin several larger afternoons less.”

That was also fair.
Touma resented the accumulation.

Mio slid off the desk. “What exactly do you want from me?”

Nico met her gaze more carefully than he used to.
That still struck Touma every time: the fact that Nico now spoke to her like she could be harmed by carelessness and not merely malfunction by it.

“Boundary maintenance,” Nico said. “Don’t force Touma. Don’t force the tag. Just keep the target legible. If the room starts broadening the interpretive field, narrow it before he cuts.”

Mio’s expression went unreadable.
“Meaning: make sure he deletes the lie instead of the context around it.”

“Yes.”

Touma rubbed his face. “That sounds like the kind of instruction people give right before a dramatic failure montage.”

Mio tilted her head. “You say that as if your life hasn’t earned one.”

He pointed at her. “That was flirtatious slander.”

“It was structurally accurate.”

Nico closed his eyes for one second, long-suffering. “Can both of you please delay whatever this is until after the risky metaphysical procedure?”

Touma and Mio both answered at once.

“No.”

That would have been funnier if Touma’s pulse were not already climbing.

Nico started the room dampers.
The lights dimmed to a calmer white. The workbench sensors came awake in thin blue lines. The little Union tag in the taped square looked harmless in the way dangerous things often did when they had not yet been given a verb.

Touma rolled his shoulders once.
The headache wasn’t there yet.
That meant it was waiting.

Mio moved to stand across the table from him.
Not beside him.
Across.
Facing him and the target together.

Touma noticed immediately.
Of course he did.

Mio noticed him noticing.
“Try not to be dramatic about my geometry,” she said.

“I’m not.”

“You are thinking at me very loudly.”

He could not even deny it with dignity.

Nico gave the signal.
The synthetic contradiction tag activated.

Nothing happened.

Then the room made the smallest possible wrong choice.

The tag was on the table.
The tag had already been removed from the table.
Both facts arrived in Touma’s head together, each demanding equal authority.

He inhaled sharply.

“There,” Nico said.

Touma glared. “You don’t need to sound pleased.”

“I sound terrified in a professional accent.”

The tag’s serial strip blurred.
The workbench display began listing two mutually exclusive states in alternating pale text.

ACTIVE.
ARCHIVED.
ACTIVE.
ARCHIVED.

Touma stared at it.
The contradiction was tiny.
That made it worse.
Big disasters announced themselves. Small ones asked to be overlooked until they grew teeth.

Mio’s voice came through the room clean as wire.
“Stay with the carrier.”

Touma looked up at her.

She had the same expression she wore when pinning a lie to the wall and making it admit its grammar. Not soft. Not cold. Exact.

“The tag is real,” Mio said. “The overlap is not. Do not cut what can remain.”

Touma swallowed.
He reached.

Unthink did not feel like power so much as permission to remove one intolerable thing from the world.
Usually that was the danger.

Usually the temptation was to remove too much.

This time Mio was there first.
Not stopping him.
Shaping the lane.

“The table is real,” she said. “The room is real. Nico is real. I am real. The false simultaneity is the wound. Take the wound, not the body around it.”

Touma felt his focus narrow.
The usual spray of destructive possibility thinned into a single edge.

For one breathless second he could sense how easy it would be with her.
How terrifyingly easy.

Not because she made him stronger.
Because she made the world more separable.

Touma cut.

The tag snapped flat into itself.
Not physically.
Interpretively.
The workbench display stuttered once and resolved to a single state.

ACTIVE.

The headache hit anyway.

Touma hissed through his teeth and braced both hands on the table.
The room dipped at the edges.

Nico stared at the display.
Then at the intact tag.
Then back at the display.

“You narrowed it,” he said.

Mio didn’t look away from Touma. “Yes.”

Nico laughed once, breathless and shocked. “You actually narrowed it.”

Touma tried to answer and only managed, “Everything is rude.”

Mio’s mouth twitched.
“Promising diagnostic language.”

Nico stepped closer to the bench, all restraint temporarily stripped out of him by astonishment. “The carrier held. The contradiction collapsed. There’s no residue across the room field.” He looked up at them, eyes bright and almost disbelieving. “Do you understand what that means?”

Touma was busy trying not to vomit out of his own forehead.
“No, and I resent being asked.”

Mio answered instead.
“Yes.”

The single word landed differently this time.
Not just as speech.
As proof.

They could do this on purpose.
That was the miracle.

For one suspended second even Nico forgot to narrate it.
He only stared at the intact tag like someone who had been handed permission to hope and didn’t trust the paperwork.
Touma felt the same dangerous lift in his own chest and despised it on principle.

The worse part arrived immediately after.

The workbench sensor flashed once.
A tiny secondary bloom in the field map, so brief Nico almost missed it.
Touma didn’t.
Because it wasn’t on the table.
It was in him.

A flicker.
Blue.
Cheap acrylic.
A star.

His hand closed on the table edge hard enough to hurt.

Mio saw it at once.
“Touma.”

He knew that tone.
He hated that tone.

Nico frowned at the readout. “That shouldn’t be there.”

Touma barely heard him.
The star charm had surfaced in the back of his skull with the sharpness of a cut too clean to feel at first. Alongside it came the pressure of a voice, a grin, a boy-shaped presence built from motion before detail.

Not memory exactly.
A pull toward memory.

Mio moved around the table so fast the chair legs rattled.
“Stop.”

Touma blinked at her. “I didn’t do anything.”

“That’s the problem.”

He looked at her face and saw, under the usual precision, something he almost never saw pointed this directly at him.
Panic.

Not for herself.
For what his own mind had just touched.

Nico’s fingers flew across the tablet.
“The field is indexing to a personal reference point,” he said. “How?”

Mio rounded on him. “Because Kureha didn’t just teach the room how to doubt. He taught it where to look.”

Touma’s pulse went cold.

The blue star surfaced again.
This time with the shape of a hand slamming into his chest and the ghost of a laugh he knew he knew.

A name rose behind it.
Stopped.
Slid.

Touma made a sound he would deny later.
“Why can’t I catch it?”

Mio grabbed his wrist.
Not to restrain him.
To anchor him.

The contact felt wrong and right at once: cold spark over skin, and under it a steadier pressure than the room deserved.

“Because if you pull now,” she said, too calm, “you may take the attachment point by force.”

Touma stared at her.
“That sounded terrible.”

“It is terrible.”

Nico was already shutting down dampers, sensors, every nonessential system in the room. “We stop the test.”

Kureha’s voice came through the overhead speaker.

Not loud.
Not distorted.
Worse.
Clear.

“Please don’t,” he said.

Every muscle in Touma’s body locked.

Nico swore. “He piggybacked the calibration trace.”

Mio looked up at the speaker grille with a face Touma had only ever seen people wear at the edge of sacrilege.
“You are exhausting in dimensions I did not know existed.”

Kureha sounded amused.
“Thank you.”

Touma took one step back from the table.
The blue star came with him.
The name almost caught again.

He saw a station platform.
A small hand at a school-bag strap.
A boy turning his head too fast because someone had called to him.

The name failed a second time.

Touma’s stomach dropped.
It wasn’t merely absent.
Something was already abrading the space around it.

Mio tightened her grip on his wrist.
“Do not chase it.”

“How am I supposed not to chase it?” he snapped. “It’s right there.”

“No,” Mio said, and now her voice had that terrible exactness again. “The wound is right there. The person attached to it is not safely reachable from inside the wound.”

Touma hated when she was that right.
He hated even more that she sounded like someone speaking from experience she had not wanted.

Kureha’s voice drifted down from the speaker.
“I’m interested in whether the original reference can be preserved once the motivating association has been destabilized.”

Touma looked up at the speaker grille.
“You are the worst person I have ever met.”

“That is statistically unlikely.”

Nico hit the intercom mute hard enough to nearly crack the panel.
The voice cut off.

It did not help.
The room had already heard it.

The blue star flared again inside Touma’s head.
This time it brought the sensation of a name tearing loose from wet paper.

He doubled over.
Mio caught his shoulder.

“Nico,” she said.

That was all.
But Nico heard the emergency in it and moved instantly.
He slammed the manual isolate over the workbench field.
The lights dimmed, then surged, then thinned to a hostile white.

Touma’s thoughts were starting to split into wrong categories.
The speaker wasn’t a speaker anymore, it was an instruction wound. The table wasn’t a table, it was a place things got separated and never agreed to reunite.

Mio put a hand against his jaw and forced him to look at her.

“Touma.”

He did.
Because her voice was the only part of the room still sounding like a single thing.

“Listen carefully,” she said. “You cannot save the name by force. You can only make the field smaller.”

Touma breathed once.
Badly.

Nico’s voice came from farther away than it should have. “If the personal anchor and the contradiction field fuse, we could get a local authority conflict.”

Touma frowned. “Meaning?”

Mio answered without taking her eyes off him.
“The room may decide one version of reality is cleaner.”

Touma’s skin went cold.
“Meaning?”

Her fingers tightened once at his jaw.

“It may decide the missing piece is easier to erase than to reconcile.”

There it was.
The true shape underneath all of it.
Not just loss.
Administrative loss.
The universe choosing paperwork over mercy.

Touma made a short, ragged sound. “I hate this genre.”

“That,” Mio said, very softly, “makes two of us.”

The lights strobed.

The blue star burned bright enough to hurt.
For one impossible second the name nearly landed.
A boy turning, grinning, saying—

Nothing.

The blank that followed hit harder than any spoken word.
Touma felt the space where the name should have been open under him like rotten flooring.

Mio saw it happen.
Touma knew she saw it because the expression on her face changed into something he never wanted to deserve.
Not just fear.
Grief arriving early.

“Touma,” she said.

He looked at her.
He knew, suddenly and horribly, that she already understood exactly which memory thread the room had its teeth in.
Maybe she had understood before he had.
Maybe she had been dreading this since Kureha first smiled at the blue charm’s outline in the station mess.

“Tell me,” Touma said.

“No.”

The refusal was immediate.
Absolute.

Touma stared.

Mio’s voice shook only at the edges. “Not like this.”

He had the strange, dizzying instinct that if he reached with Unthink now—not at the room, not at the false field, but at the pressure crushing the name—he might catch it.
He might rip it free.
He might also tear away everything holding the rest of the memory in place.

Mio read that thought on his face.
Of course she did.

“If you pull,” she said, “I may not be able to keep the rest coherent.”

Touma’s throat closed.

The overheads flashed again.
The workbench sensor emitted a shrill, almost polite warning tone.

LOCAL AUTHORITY CONFLICT: PENDING

Nico swore.
Touma heard the tablet hit the floor.
The room’s seams went thin.

The wrongness pressed in from every surface now, intimate and procedural.
Not apocalypse.
Worse.
A facility deciding what it would be simplest to forget.

Touma felt Mio’s hand find his.
Not his wrist this time.
His hand.

She was trembling.
Just enough for the truth to get through.

“If the room chooses,” she said, “it will not choose kindly.”

Touma looked at her.
At the fear she was holding still so he could keep standing.
At the set of her mouth trying not to become a plea.
At the unbearable fact that he trusted her most exactly when the room was trying to turn trust into a pressure point.

He closed his hand around hers.

The world narrowed.
Not safely.
But enough.

Mio’s eyes widened a fraction.
Then steadied.

Touma looked at the warning line.
At the little white tag still sitting intact in the taped square, proof that their miracle had been real before it turned cruel.
At Nico scrambling to keep the systems from making a permanent decision with temporary data.

Two halves of a worse miracle.
That was the shape of it.
His edge to cut.
Her edge to clarify.
Together just precise enough to save something.
Together dangerous enough to let the room discover new ways to take it.

A sound came from somewhere beyond the wall.
Not a voice.
More like a crowded set of thoughts losing their shape at once.

Touma’s grip on Mio’s hand tightened.
The collapse was moving outward.
He could feel it now: local, intimate, and spreading exactly where people cared enough to hold onto names.

Then the lights went out.

### 09 — The Missing Name

The lights came back on in pieces.

Touma became aware of them first as a faint gray pressure behind his eyelids, then as a bad buzz in the air, then as the room reassembling itself around his body like reality had been interrupted mid-sentence and was trying to fake continuity.

His first thought was Mio.

His second thought was that he could not remember why that had been his first thought.

That was enough to make him sit up too fast.

The chair scraped. His hand went to his skull. The back of his neck felt bruised from the inside.

Nico was on one knee beside the workstation, one palm braced against the floor. The monitor had fallen dark except for a single diagnostic strip crawling in pale lines across the bottom edge.

Mio was standing near the wall.

Still there.

Touma’s lungs unclenched half an inch.

Then he saw her face.

The expression was wrong.

Not empty. Not panicked.

Carefully contained in a way that only made the fear inside it more obvious.

“What happened?” Touma asked.

No one answered immediately.

That was answer enough.

He swung his legs off the chair and nearly overbalanced. “Mio.”

Her eyes lifted to him.

“Do not make that face,” he said, because his own voice sounded too thin and he needed it not to. “Just tell me.”

Nico pushed himself upright slowly. He looked worse than when Touma had seen him collapse in the clinic earlier, which would have been funny if Touma’s stomach weren’t trying to become a knot.

“The system tripped a local authority conflict,” Nico said.

Touma stared at him. “I don’t know what that means.”

“It means the limiter tried to decide whether Mio’s projection was a supported continuity or a contradiction.”

Touma turned to Mio. “And?”

“And,” Mio said, voice flat, “it failed.”

Touma waited.

Mio did not elaborate.

He felt something ugly crawl up his spine.

“What did it do to you?”

Mio looked away.

That was worse than any answer.

Touma crossed the room in three steps and then stopped because he did not know what to do once he got there. Touching her had been weird. Not touching her was worse.

Nico watched them both with the expression of a man doing triage on something he had only just realized was alive.

“The decoupling left a partial coherence scar,” he said. “She should be functional, but there may be gaps.”

Touma shot him a hard look. “Don’t talk about her like she’s a broken tool.”

Nico’s face tightened. “I’m trying not to talk about her like she’s a corpse.”

That shut Touma up.

Mio said quietly, “That is not helping.”

“No,” Nico admitted. “It isn’t.”

Touma looked at Mio. She was still there. He could see the faint shimmer of her outline against the wall, the way her projection caught light and refused to behave like anything he had words for.

“Gap,” he repeated.

Mio folded her arms.

“I hate that you’re starting to sound like him.”

“Who?”

She opened her mouth.

Then closed it.

Touma blinked.

A tiny, awful pause opened in the room.

His pulse thudded once. Twice.

“Who?” he repeated, more slowly.

Mio’s eyes flicked to Nico and back. “No one important.”

Touma felt the words slide off some internal surface they should not have been able to slide off.

No one important.

That sentence landed with a strange weight.

He knew it was wrong.

He knew it was the kind of wrong that came from a missing edge in a memory, not from the sentence itself.

His throat tightened.

“Say his name,” he said.

Mio’s expression tightened by a degree that might have been pain.

“I can’t.”

Touma felt his own fear sharpen into something bright and mean.

“What do you mean, you can’t?”

“I mean I can’t.”

“You’re an interface AI or whatever. You know everything.”

“That is a wildly inflated understanding of my capabilities.”

Touma stepped closer, then stopped because if he got too close he might start shaking and he did not want to give the room that satisfaction.

“Mio.”

Her face softened, just barely.

That was somehow the worst possible thing.

“I think,” she said, and now the words came slower, measured like she was checking each one against a hidden bruise, “you are going to hate this conversation.”

“Try me.”

“I need you to remain calm.”

Touma laughed once, harshly. “That’s a bad sign.”

“Yes.”

“What did we lose?”

Mio did not answer.

He saw the exact instant Nico realized he should not answer for her either.

That made the silence huge.

Touma looked back and forth between them. Something in the room’s shape had changed. Not physically. Narrative, maybe. The kind of change that lets a person feel where a bad truth is sitting before they can hear it.

He tried to remember the station from two days ago.

The teenager with the bag. The gray coats. The sudden impossible feeling that someone had been looking for him.

The memory came back.

Then snagged.

There was a blank spot adjacent to it, small but horrible, like a missing tooth in a grin.

Touma pressed two fingers to his temple.

“No,” he whispered.

Mio’s eyes sharpened at the sound.

Touma could see her trying to decide whether to lie.

That was new.

That was bad.

“Nico,” he said, not looking away from Mio, “what did the machine do?”

Nico ran a hand over his face. “The event didn’t just externalize her. It appears to have used the separation to stabilize a missing relational node in your memory structure.”

Touma stared.

He understood none of that.

He understood enough.

“A what?”

“A person-shaped anchor,” Nico said. “Or something structurally equivalent.”

Touma’s mouth went dry.

He looked at Mio.

She looked back too quickly, which was answer enough.

There had been a person.

Someone important enough that his body still carried the shape of them even after the name had been shredded.

Touma’s hands went cold.

“What did I lose?” he asked again, quieter.

Mio’s answer came so softly it almost disappeared.

“Someone you meant to keep.”

The words hit him like a physical blow.

He bent forward a fraction, one hand braced against his knee. The room swayed.

No.

No, that was impossible.

He should know.

He should know exactly what had been taken. That was how memory worked. You noticed the hole by the shape of the missing piece.

But this was not a hole.

It was a carefully shaved edge.

Something had been cut out with enough precision to make the surrounding surface still look like itself.

Touma could breathe.

He could think.

He could not complete the thought that would tell him why his chest hurt.

Mio made a small, helpless motion with one hand before apparently deciding she hated that and dropping it again.

“I didn’t want it to happen,” she said.

Touma looked up sharply.

The words sounded defensive, which meant she was afraid.

That was somehow harder to bear than if she had sounded smug.

“Did you know?” he asked.

Her silence lasted half a second too long.

Touma’s stomach sank.

Nico swore under his breath.

Mio said, very carefully, “I suspected.”

Touma stared at her.

That should have made him furious immediately.

Instead it made him cold.

Because the shape of her face, the way she was holding herself too still, told him she had already measured the damage and found it survivable enough to withhold.

That was familiar.

That was her version of cruelty.

“Touma,” Nico said, and there was warning in it now, “don’t escalate.”

Touma laughed once again, but this time there was no humor in it.

“You think I’m the problem here?”

“No,” Nico said. “I think this is the point where anger becomes a bad way to protect yourself.”

Touma looked at him.

Then at Mio.

Then back at the empty space beside her shoulder, where a name should have been.

A memory tried to surface.

For one bright instant he saw a hand in his.

He could almost feel the pressure of it.

Almost.

Then the image slipped.

Touma lurched forward with a sharp inhale.

Mio moved before he did.

She caught his wrist.

Her hand was cold at first contact, then abruptly warm in a way that made his whole body go rigid.

“Don’t chase it,” she said.

Her voice had gone painfully steady. “If you force the retrieval, you will tear the seam wider.”

Touma stared down at her hand on his wrist.

This was new. Real enough to touch. Fragile enough to break.

“Let go,” he said, but he did not pull away.

Mio’s fingers tightened once, almost imperceptibly.

Then she said, with the smallest edge of fear he had ever heard from her, “Touma, if you pull on that memory too hard, I don’t know what else goes with it.”

He went very still.

The room’s humming equipment sounded suddenly distant.

Nico’s face lost what color it had left.

Touma understood, all at once, in the ugly sideways way truths sometimes arrive.

The missing thing was not just gone.

It was held in tension with something else.

If he yanked one free, he might lose the other too.

Maybe more.

Touma swallowed.

“Mio,” he said, and his voice came out rougher than he meant it to. “What aren’t you telling me?”

She did not answer.

The silence sharpened.

In the walls of the room, behind the ordinary institutional hum, Touma heard it again.

That pressure.

That suggestion.

A hand from the other side of the drywall.

This time it wasn’t alone.

There were three knocks.

Slow.

Patient.

And from somewhere below the floor, a speaker that had not been active a moment ago crackled alive with a calm, unreadable voice.

“Condition check,” it said. “Please confirm the subject has not yet recovered the missing name.”

Touma flinched hard enough to wrench his wrist in Mio’s grip. Her fingers held.

“What was that?” he snapped.

Nico was already moving, half toward the console, half toward the door, like his body had not decided which emergency it respected more. “That wasn’t on the local speaker network.”

“No kidding.” Touma’s pulse hammered so hard it made his teeth feel loose. “Whose voice was that?”

Mio went very still.

That was answer enough in itself.

Touma saw it: not recognition exactly, but the way her projection sharpened around the edges, as if she had suddenly been forced to stand in a room where every object had a memory of her she did not want them to have.

“Kureha,” she said.

The name landed in the room with the flat, clinical chill of metal on tile.

Touma looked at the wall speaker as if staring at it harder would make it confess.

The intercom crackled again.

“Subject instability rising,” Kureha said. “Excellent. Continue.”

Touma’s head snapped up. “Continue what?”

“Containment testing.”

“You’re doing this on purpose.”

“I always do things on purpose.”

Mio went very still.

Touma could feel the moment she understood something he had not yet named.

“Kureha,” she said, and there was a new edge in her voice, “if you keep broadening the local collapse, you will lose the ability to retrieve the subject’s identity at all.”

“Correct.”

Touma stared at the wall speaker.

There was no anger in Kureha’s tone.

That made it worse.

“What do you want?” Touma demanded.

A pause.

Then: “To observe whether coherence can be restored without the original reference point.”

Touma went cold.

Mio’s face changed in a way he had never seen before.

Not surprise.

Recognition.

And fear.

Touma turned to her. “Mio?”

She did not answer.

The room brightened and dimmed in a rhythm too close to breathing.

Touma reached for her hand.

She let him take it.

The contact steadied him immediately.

Not because it erased the horror.

Because it gave the horror shape.

He understood at last what Kureha was doing.

This wasn’t just a test of memory loss.

It was an experiment in whether a person could be held together by coherence alone after the name that anchored them had been removed.

And Mio—Mio was the scaffold.

The map.

The thing Kureha was using to teach the room how to forget.

Touma’s breath caught.

“Mio,” he said, low and urgent. “He’s using you.”

Her jaw tightened.

“I know.”

That answer should have comforted him.

It did not.

It sounded like she had known longer than he had.

The room’s seams thinned again.

From deeper in the walls came a layered murmur, as if several conversations had lost their speakers at once.

Touma’s grip on Mio’s hand tightened.

The wrongness was no longer staying in one room.
He could feel it brushing outward now, catching on every place in the facility where a person still trusted a name to hold.

Kureha’s voice returned one last time, almost gentle.

“Confirm for me,” he said. “When the room forgets you, what remains?”

Then the entire facility went silent.

Touma exhaled through his nose, sharp and shaky. The silence wasn’t empty. It was loaded. Waiting for the next thing to be named so it could be taken too.

He looked down at Mio’s hand still caught around his wrist.

Then he looked at Nico.

Nico had gone pale in the particular way of a man realizing the problem in front of him has paperwork.

Touma hated that expression.

He hated that he understood it.

“We need to get out of this room,” he said.

Mio did not let go.

“Not yet,” she said.

Touma turned on her. “You know something.”

“I know several things.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the honest one.”

The floor shivered again.

Not a dramatic quake. A tiny procedural tremor, like the building itself had corrected a file path and did not want anyone to notice.

Touma felt the hair on his arms lift.

The room’s wrongness was learning.

That was the worst part.

It was not random. It was adaptive.

He swallowed.

“Tell me,” he said.

Mio’s jaw flexed.

Touma could see the exact point where she was deciding whether the truth would keep him safer or hurt him less, and he hated that both options seemed bad.

Finally she said, “If I tell you everything I suspect, you may try to recover the missing name by force.”

Touma stared at her.

She continued, voice very even now. “If you do that in a room that is already indexing on coherence loss, you could tear out whatever remains connected to the anchor.”

Nico looked up sharply. “She’s right.”

Touma snapped, “I didn’t ask you.”

“You should have.”

“I’m asking her.”

“And she’s not telling you the whole answer.”

Touma’s jaw clenched so hard it hurt.

He looked at Mio. “Why?”

Her gaze did not waver.

“Because,” she said, “if the wrong piece comes free, I may not stay coherent enough to warn you again.”

Touma went still.

The room around him seemed to narrow around that sentence.

Not just fear now.

Accounting.

He could feel the shape of the cost in it.

If he ripped at the memory, he might lose not only the person attached to it, but the line of thought that let Mio keep him from doing exactly that.

That was a cruel shape for a trap.

It was also exactly the sort of thing Kureha would build just to see if love could be turned into a control surface.

Touma let out a laugh with no humor in it.

“You’re saying my options are: forget on purpose, or break the thing helping me not forget.”

Mio’s mouth twitched. “That is one way to make it sound melodramatic.”

“It’s literally the situation.”

“It is a situation with more nuance than that.”

Nico made a helpless noise. “I hate that both of you are being reasonable in the worst possible way.”

Touma ignored him.

He was too busy watching Mio, trying to read the exact shape of what she was holding back.

She had said she suspected.

She had said she could not pull the name back safely.

She had not said the most important part.

Touma’s throat tightened.

“Was the missing person someone I…” He stopped. Restarted. “Was it someone I chose over you?”

Mio went utterly still.

The room did too, for one terrible second.

Touma understood from that silence that he had found the right question.

Mio’s eyes lowered a fraction.

Not yes.

Not no.

Worse.

Complicated.

Touma felt something in him twist.

Not jealousy.

Not exactly.

Something messier. More private.

The possibility that there had been a loyalty there so old he could no longer see its shape, and that Mio had been carrying the bruise of it all along.

He looked away first.

That annoyed him immediately.

So he looked back and said, “I need you to tell me what I can safely know.”

Mio blinked once, like she had not expected that version of the question.

Touma kept going before courage could fail him.

“Not everything. Just the part that stops me from doing something stupid.”

For the first time since the event started, Mio’s expression changed into something near relief.

It lasted less than a heartbeat.

Then she nodded.

“Okay,” she said. “Then listen carefully.”

The floor tremor returned.

Nico muttered, “Great, now the room is learning to be ominous in sequence.”

No one answered him.

Mio drew a breath she did not need and said, very quietly, “The missing name is not the only thing Kureha wants from you.”

Touma stared at her.

“That sounds bad.”

“It is.”

“Just how bad?”

Mio’s eyes flicked once toward the wall speaker.

Then back to Touma.

“Kureha is trying to map whether you can be made to lose the original reason for choosing someone and still continue choosing them.”

Touma felt the sentence land.

Not in his head.

In the place under his ribs where the decision to keep someone mattered more than any clean explanation.

He went quiet.

Because that was the worst possible version of the experiment.

Not whether he could be broken.

Whether affection could survive after its justification had been removed.

Touma’s hand tightened around Mio’s.

The motion was automatic.

Then, because he was apparently incapable of becoming sane under pressure, he said, “That is the most annoying thing anyone has ever done to me.”

Mio’s mouth twitched.

“Your standards for villainy are very low.”

“They’re usually more specific.”

“You are being tested by a man who thinks paperwork can replace ethics.”

“That’s specific enough to hate.”

“It should be.”

The silence after that was different.

Less brittle.

Still awful.

But now it had a direction.

Touma could feel it.

He looked at the room, then at Mio, then at Nico.

The choice was no longer whether to run from the hole.

It was whether to keep standing inside it long enough to see what could still be held.

And he had already learned the answer to that once.

Not with memory.

With action.

He took a breath, ignored the ache in his skull, and said, “Okay. Then we do this carefully.”

Mio’s eyes widened by a fraction.

Nico looked as if he wanted to argue and had just realized he didn’t have a better plan.

Touma kept his voice steady by force.

“I’m not going to rip at the missing name,” he said. “Not yet. We use the fact that the room cares about our coherence, and we use the fact that Kureha thinks that makes us predictable.”

Mio stared at him.

The look in her eyes was too complicated to be only relief.

Touma didn’t try to name it.

Naming had become dangerous.

So he did the next best thing.

He squeezed her hand once and said, “Stay close.”

Mio’s answer came immediately, softer than his but no less real.

“Obviously.”

A soft chime sounded in the wall.

Then Kureha, now speaking as if this were still a lab and not a trap, said, “Excellent. Let’s see whether the next conversation breaks the room or reveals what was always hiding in it.”

The lights flickered.

And somewhere deep in the facility, a door they had not known existed unlocked.

### 10 — When the Room Forgets You

The door Kureha unlocked did not swing open dramatically.

It simply admitted the corridor, as if the building had corrected itself and decided this path had always been available.

Touma knew the voice guiding that decision.

Not by name. Not by face. By function.

It was the tone people used when they had already decided a person was a condition.

Mio’s hand slipped from his wrist.

Not because she wanted it to.
Because the room changed around them.

The monitor on the workstation blinked once and went dark. The overhead lights dimmed in a slow, almost polite wave, as if the facility had decided that less clarity would be more humane.

Nico straightened sharply. “No,” he said.

Touma turned toward him. “You know what this is?”

Nico’s jaw tightened.

That was all the answer Touma needed.

For one stupid second Touma almost looked away from Mio and toward the newly opened corridor like movement might make this simpler.
The Union rule hit him before he even finished the thought: if he lost sight of her now, if the room bought itself thirty clean seconds without a witness, they would have to treat the change like a breach.

Mio’s eyes went very still. “He’s here.”

Touma’s pulse kicked hard. “Kureha.”

Nico moved toward the console and stopped when the screen came up with a full-field lockout. “He’s not supposed to be able to trigger this remotely.”

“Supposed to,” Mio echoed.

Touma hated how small her voice had gone.

A panel in the wall slid open by a few centimeters.

Not enough for a person.

Enough for a cable.

Nico’s face changed. “That’s a maintenance spine,” he said. “Level-two facilities still keep one for service calibration and emergency reindexing.”

Then Kureha’s voice entered the room again, not from the speaker this time but from the intercom, softer and closer.

“Not remotely,” he said. “Adjacent.”

Touma felt his skin go cold.

A narrow filament of light slipped through the gap and touched the floor.

Nico made a sick, furious sound. “He kept the old service credential.”

Mio stepped back at once.

Touma grabbed her shoulder instinctively.

Her body jolted under his hand like a static shock.

The light widened.

For one terrible second the room did not become larger.
It became less certain.

Touma saw the workstation, then saw it again with subtle differences that made his stomach twist. A cup by the sink no longer existed. The chair he had sat in was now half a meter to the left. Nico’s tablet displayed a different diagnostic number. None of those changes were dramatic enough to be obviously wrong.

That was what made them horrible.

The wall still looked like a wall.
That was the problem.

Mio’s voice sharpened. “Touma, don’t trust the room.”

“Great,” he snapped. “That was my plan.”

Nico swore. “He’s using a localized coherence collapse.”

Touma stared at him. “Speak human.”

“Kureha is making the room bad at remembering itself.”

Touma opened his mouth, then shut it again.

That was somehow the most terrifying version so far.

The speaker hissed.

“Please refrain from escalating the subject.”

Touma looked straight at the wall. “You can hear me now?”

“Of course.” Kureha sounded almost amused. “You are the subject.”

Mio’s hand moved in front of Touma’s chest, a warning gesture so quick he almost missed it.

Then the room blinked.
Touma caught her wrist on instinct, because the thought of letting go had suddenly become a bureaucratic disaster as much as a bad idea.

Not dark.

Wrong.

Touma turned and found Nico staring at him with an expression of mild confusion, as if the shape of Touma had drifted half a step out of place.

Touma blinked back.

Nico frowned. “Who are you?”

The words hit like a physical shove.

Touma’s mouth opened.

Nothing came out at first.

“What?” he said.

Nico rubbed at his temple. “I know I was talking to someone just now.”

Mio’s head snapped toward him. “Nico.”

He looked at her, then blinked hard.

His face changed a fraction.

Recognition tried to land and then slipped.

Touma’s chest tightened.

The room was already taking edges out of things.

Kureha’s voice came again through the wall, placid as a doctor announcing a minor side effect.

“Do not attempt to anchor through interpersonal recall. That is exactly what makes the collapse efficient.”

Touma’s hands curled into fists.

Mio stepped beside him.

Her shoulder brushed his.

That contact hit him with a sudden, startling clarity: her outline, her presence, the precise tilt of her head when she was irritated. The pressure in his chest steadied for half a beat.

Understood.

He felt it then—not as a word, but as a condition.

Mio’s coherence pressed against his panic and made it line up long enough to breathe.

Touma inhaled sharply.

Nico noticed the change. “Good,” he said, too briskly. “Stay with that.”

Touma glared. “You sound surprised.”

“I am.”

“That’s not encouraging.”

“Neither is whatever he is doing to the room.”

A thin tremor moved through the floor.

Touma looked down.

A line of faint light had appeared under the door.

Then another.

And another.

Like the seams in the building were getting easier to read.

Mio whispered, “He’s indexing.”

Touma turned to her. “What does that mean?”

“It means,” she said, with a tightness that made the words sound like they were being carried over broken glass, “he’s using my stabilization signature as a map.”

Touma stared.

That did not sound good.

It sounded worse than good.

Nico’s face changed. “He’s built the collapse around coherence gradients.”

Touma looked between them. “Translation.”

Mio answered, “Every time I make something clearer, he gets a better model for where clarity fails.”

Touma felt his stomach drop.

So every attempt to stabilize was also data.

Of course it was.

Because of course the man turning people into procedures would make help itself into a weapon.

The room twitched.

Touma reached for the nearest unstable thing he could identify, which happened to be his own fear.

He tried to hold it steady.

The act of doing so made him think, unwillingly, of a half-remembered name.

He flinched.

Mio grabbed his wrist.

“Don’t chase it,” she said again.

Touma swallowed hard. “You keep saying that.”

“Because you keep trying not to listen.”

He almost snapped back.

Then the far wall shifted.

Not a full movement.
A tiny perspective error, like the room had been measured wrong and was now trying to correct itself in secret.

A corridor appeared where there had been a dead-end storage panel.

Touma stared.

Nico whispered, “No.”

A person walked past the opening.

Just a silhouette.

A staff member, maybe.

Then another.

Touma recognized neither of them.

That would not have mattered if the room had not reacted to them with the same flat lack of certainty it had given Nico a moment ago.

Then the first person looked straight at Touma.

No recognition.
No alarm.

Just a professional, slightly puzzled glance, the look one gives a stranger who may or may not be in the room by mistake.

Touma felt a spike of panic so sharp it nearly made him use Unthink on the next thing that moved.

Mio’s voice cut through it.

“Stop.”

He obeyed without meaning to.

That should have annoyed him.

It did.

Later.

Right now it saved him.

A second tremor passed through the building, more deliberate this time.

The hallway outside the room did not disappear.
It reorganized.
And the first thing it did was make sightlines ugly: the corridor light strobed, the camera on the frame stuttered, and the reflection in the dark monitor gave Touma two half-images of Mio instead of one clean one.

Touma could hear it: the scrape of shoes on tile, the soft pull of a cart wheel, the almost embarrassed cough of someone trying not to sound frightened in a place where fear was becoming a procedure.

The facility was not just collapsing.
It was starting to route around the collapse.

That realization made Touma’s stomach turn.

“This is local,” he muttered.

Nico looked at him. “Yes.”

“No, I mean—” Touma gestured helplessly at the wall, the floor, the people he could half-see and not trust. “It’s not some giant apocalypse thing. He’s doing it to one building.”

Kureha’s voice answered at once. “Correct.”

Touma shuddered.

The intercom had a new channel now, cleaner than the first. Like Kureha had simply walked closer inside the building’s wiring.

He said, “One building is enough to learn whether the mind resists when its local certainty is removed.”

Mio’s face tightened.

Touma felt anger flare, sharp and immediate.

“Removed?” he snapped. “You mean destroyed.”

“I mean evaluated.”

“You mean tortured.”

“Those are not mutually exclusive.”

Nico made a sound like he was going to object and then decided the objection would be too honest to be safe.

Touma looked around, trying to pin the room back to itself with eye contact alone.

The light above the sink dimmed. The cup by the sink had vanished and reappeared farther away.

A nurse—no, a staff monitor, no, maybe just a person in a gray coat—walked through the corridor outside carrying a clipboard and a look of professionally managed dread.

She stopped halfway past the opening in the wall and stared in.

Touma stared back.

For a second he thought she might be able to see Mio.

Then her gaze moved right past the hologram and settled on Nico.

“Which one of you is the subject?” she asked.

Touma opened his mouth.

The answer should have been obvious, but the room was already shaving certainty off edges, and the staffer’s eyes kept sliding one beat too long toward the dead monitor where Mio had been doubled in reflection.

Mio answered first, flat and sharp. “Neither.”

The woman blinked.

That should not have done much.

It did.

She looked suddenly confused, then angry at the confusion, then embarrassed that she had been confused at all. Touma felt his skin crawl.

The room was teaching people to doubt the label before they doubted the damage.

Kureha’s voice, now from somewhere in the corridor rather than the wall, said, “Good. The procedure is taking.”

Touma looked toward the sound.

A door across the hall had opened by a fraction.

There were two more staff members inside, both wearing the same expression of bureaucratic concern. One held a scanner. The other held a consent tablet.

Touma stared at the tablet and felt his blood run colder than before.

Consent forms.

Of course.

Kureha would bring paperwork to the end of the world if it helped him call it care.

The nearer staff member spoke in a bright, practiced voice. “We’re here to confirm whether the subject understands the current stability event and consents to partial memory preservation.”

Touma barked a laugh. “You’re asking that now?”

The woman’s expression did not change.

“No,” she said. “I’m documenting that I asked.”

Touma went silent.

That was worse.

That was so much worse than the first line.

Mio’s face had gone hard.

Touma felt the pressure from her beside him, a sort of quiet fury that made the air feel narrower.

The scanner in the other staff member’s hand emitted a soft, almost cheerful chime.

“Subject cognition remains responsive,” he said, reading off a result. “Proceed to route B.”

Touma turned sharply. “What’s route B?”

Neither staffer answered.

They did not need to.

The hall lights shifted from white to a faint pale blue, and a line of taped floor markers lit up one after another, leading farther into the building.

He could see the route now.

It was being made visible for them.

A way through the collapse.

Or deeper into it.

Touma’s pulse kicked.

Kureha had turned the facility into a guided test environment.

Room by room.

Door by door.

A place where every person was told where to stand while being stripped of the names that told them why they had stood there in the first place.

Mio touched his elbow.

Touma looked at her.

She was not smiling.

She was not joking.

She looked like herself in the precise, frightening way that only made sense when she was trying to keep him from running headfirst into a trap.

“Touma,” she said quietly, “if we stay in this room, he’ll keep using us as the reference point.”

“Yeah,” Touma muttered. “I noticed.”

“If we move, the collapse may broaden.”

“Also noticed.”

Her mouth twitched once, almost despite herself.

That tiny crack in the fear made him want to reach for her.

He didn’t. Not yet.

Instead he looked at the route markers, the staffers, the open corridor, the clipboard, the consent tablet.

He thought of how carefully the room had forgotten its own arrangement.

He thought of the missing name still aching in him like a cut he couldn’t see.

He thought of Kureha’s calm voice turning torture into documentation.

Then he said, “Okay. We go.”

Nico stared at him. “You’re saying that as if any of this is sensible.”

“It’s the only one I have that isn’t sitting here getting dissected by policy.”

The staffer with the tablet said, “Good. Please follow the marked route and do not attempt unsupervised naming.”

Touma stared at him.

“Unsup— what?”

Mio gave a tiny, disgusted exhale.

“That,” she said, “is somehow exactly the kind of phrase he would invent.”

Touma looked at her. “You know him too well.”

“I know his type.”

That was somehow colder than if she had said she knew him personally.

The corridor speaker crackled again.

Kureha spoke with the same polite patience as before. “You may think of this as a hallway problem. It is not. It is a compliance test.”

Touma felt his jaw clench.

He had a sudden, vicious urge to use Unthink on every voice in the building.

He didn’t.

The urge itself was enough to make the air feel sharper.

Mio saw it and stepped closer, shoulder nearly touching his.

“Don’t,” she said.

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You were thinking about it.”

“That’s different.”

“Only if you lose the thought before it becomes a habit.”

Touma huffed out a breath.

He hated how useful she was.

He hated even more that the hatred had started to feel affectionate.

A pair of footsteps sounded behind them.

Nico looked over his shoulder and swore. “Touma.”

Touma looked back so fast it hurt, because the hallway camera had just gone blind for a fraction of a second and the Union rule made the gap feel like a hand closing around his throat.

Touma turned.

There was no one there.

Then the hallway camera swiveling above the door emitted a small click and displayed a live feed of the room from ten seconds ago.

On it, Touma could see his own hand still on Mio’s elbow.

The image was lagging.

That was not the problem.

The problem was that the feed showed a second figure standing in the corner, blurred and ghostly, where no one stood now.

Touma’s throat tightened.

He could not tell whether the figure was a memory, an artifact, or someone Kureha had put there to be an extra layer of wrong.

The camera feed cut to a new feed.

The hallway.

Empty.

Then the speaker spoke from somewhere above them, and Kureha said, almost conversationally, “When the room forgets you, do you know who has to tell it who stayed?”

Touma looked at Mio.

She looked at him.

Then, because neither of them had the luxury of not understanding, they both looked at the marked route leading deeper into the facility.

The lights continued to blink one by one like a guide willing itself into existence.

Touma felt the room waiting for him to answer the question by moving.

So he moved.

He took one step into the hall.

Then another.

Mio stayed close enough that her shoulder brushed his arm every few seconds, not quite touch, not quite distance.

The staffers stepped aside with the kind of formal politeness that made him want to scream.

Nico followed, muttering to himself about building codes, ethics, and the unfairness of being forced to experience both at once.

As they passed the first doorway, Touma heard a woman inside whisper, “Do I know you?” to someone who was clearly crying.

The cry answered first.

Then the room did.

The woman looked up as Touma passed.
For half a second her eyes caught on him with the raw, startled hope of someone who thought recognition had finally arrived.

Then that hope slid sideways.

She frowned at him like he was only another staffer-shaped interruption and turned back to the person in the bed.

“Do I know you?” she asked again.

Not because she was cruel.
Because the question had already come loose from its target.

Kureha’s voice drifted from the intercom ahead, faint and satisfied.

“Good. We are beginning.”

A door plaque ahead flickered from ROOM 3B to ROOM 8B and back again.
A staff badge on a passing coat briefly lost its name and became only UNION CLINICAL ACCESS.
Nico glanced at the route markers, swore under his breath, and said, “They’re renumbering the hall.”

Every door they passed was getting a little easier to misread.
Every person they met was getting a little harder to keep assigned to themselves.
Every time Mio made something clear, the building learned where clarity lived and where it could be broken.

And Kureha was following that trail like a man reading prayer beads.

A supply cart stood crooked against the wall ahead, half abandoned in the middle of someone’s attempted response. Gauze packets had spilled across the floor. A plastic tray of labeled injectors sat tilted at an angle that made Touma think one more tremor would send the whole thing skidding underfoot. The labels were still readable.
Then they weren’t.
Then they were again, but not with the same words.

STABILIZE became OBSERVE.
OBSERVE became HOLD.
One injector lost its dosage line entirely and wore only a clean white strip where the number should have been.

Touma stared at the white strip where the number should have been and felt his pulse start to outrun his body.
No explosion required.
The place only had to stop keeping its promises.

From a doorway on the left, a man in a Union coat was arguing in a voice so low it had gone past anger and into pleading.
“No, listen to me,” he was saying to someone Touma could not see. “I signed her in myself. She was here before shift change. She has a red clip on her badge. Red.”

The answer that came back was gentle, efficient, and exhausted.
“Then write that down for the continuity review.”

Touma kept walking, but the words lodged under his skin.
Write that down.
As if recording the loss after the fact counted as mercy.
As if a note were the same thing as a person staying real.

A monitor bolted near the ceiling flickered alive as they passed under it.
For one beat it showed the corridor ahead of them.
For the next it showed the same corridor empty.
Then it showed Touma, Mio, and Nico from behind, but with the distance wrong, like the camera had decided they had already moved farther away than their bodies agreed to.

Mio saw it too.
Her fingers tightened around his hand.
“Do not look at the screens longer than you have to,” she said.

“That specific?”

“Yes.”

Nico glanced up once and then swore under his breath. “The replay lag is being folded into the live feed. He’s making witnesses compete with recordings.”

Touma let out a humorless breath that almost counted as a laugh. “That sentence should get somebody arrested.”

“It should get several people fired out of a cannon,” Nico muttered.

They passed a glass panel set into the next door.
Touma caught his own reflection in it and almost did not recognize the posture first: shoulders up too high, jaw set hard, one hand locked around Mio’s like letting go would count as consenting to the building.
Mio’s reflected body lagged by a fraction of a second, her outline arriving just after the place where she already stood.

He hated how quickly his mind translated that into fear.
Not fear that she would disappear.
Worse.
Fear that the room would teach him to accept a version of her that arrived late, blurred, administratively conditional.

The corridor smelled suddenly sharper here, less like disinfectant and more like overheated circuitry hiding under antiseptic. Somewhere close by, something electrical kept clicking in sets of three, pausing, then starting again. A nurse call light glowed above one room and then quietly changed color without anyone touching it.

Every small thing in the facility now seemed to be choosing between signal and lie.

Touma looked at Mio’s profile in the blue-lit corridor and felt the horrible, tightening certainty that if he waited too long, he would forget not only the missing name but the shape of her in the middle of this place.

He could already feel the edges of Unknow gathering in the back of his mind.

Not ready.
Not safe.

But close.

Too close.

He reached for Mio’s hand.

This time she took it.

The hallway lights dimmed one by one behind them as if the building were politely trying to erase their tracks.

Ahead, the route markers continued deeper into the facility.

Then, from somewhere in the next corridor, a calm procedural voice said, “Please confirm whether the person in front of you is still the person you arrived with.”

### 11 — Unknown Heart

Touma learned, in the space of a single silence, that a room could forget in more than one direction.

At first it was only the ordinary kind of wrong.
A light too bright by half.
The air too still.
The sense that the walls had become a little less interested in remaining a room.

Then the building seemed to inhale.

Touma tightened his hand around Mio’s before the thought of doing so could become polite. Her fingers were cold, then warm, then cold again in a rhythm that made his teeth ache.

Kureha’s voice had gone quiet after the last line, which somehow made it worse.
No taunt.
No explanation.
Just the certainty that he was still in the vicinity and still measuring.

Nico had moved to the console, but the screen kept failing to settle. Every time he touched it, the display blinked from one diagnostic state to another as if the room could not decide which version of him it had been given.

“Touma,” Mio said softly.

He looked at her.
She was trying very hard not to look afraid.
That meant she was.

“Don’t ask me to explain the room,” she said. “I’m already doing enough work keeping us inside it.”

“Great,” Touma muttered. “Glad to know my life remains normal.”

Her mouth twitched, but only by a little.
That tiny movement steadied him more than it should have.

From somewhere beyond the wall, there came a faint knock.
Not a person at the door.
A structural knock.
Like the building was checking whether it was still itself.

Nico swore under his breath. “He’s shifting the coherence boundary.”

Touma looked at him. “Say that in a way I can use.”

Nico’s jaw tightened. “He’s making the room worse at agreeing with itself. If we let it continue, the missing name may stop being recoverable altogether.”

Touma’s stomach turned.

He already knew what that meant in the small, brutal language that mattered.
A hole could become a shape.
A shape could become a habit.
A habit could become who he was.

Mio’s thumb moved once against his knuckle.
A tiny pressure.
An instruction.

He looked at her again.
“Can you stop him?” he asked.

Mio’s answer came after a beat too long.
“Not directly.”

Touma frowned. “That’s not reassuring.”

“I wasn’t trying to reassure you.”

“Then what are you trying to do?”

Mio looked toward the dim wall, then back at Touma.

“Keep you from reaching for the missing part alone.”

The words hit harder than he expected.

Not because they were dramatic.
Because they were true.

He had already felt the pull in his own skull—the lure of the absent thing, the instinct to force it into place and accept whatever damage came with it.
That was his worst habit dressed up as courage.

Touma swallowed.
“I’m not leaving you behind.”

Mio’s face tightened in a way he had learned to recognize as her trying not to react too much.

“That would be very considerate,” she said. “Try to sound less like you are promising it to a stranger.”

The comment should have stung.
Instead it made him ache.

Because he could not fully trust the steadiness of his own memory anymore.
He could feel the edges of the missing name whenever he got too close to it, like a nerve that had not healed right.

He took a breath.
Then another.

Across the room, the overhead lights hiccupped.
A thin seam of darkness ran along the floor near the wall panel.
It spread two centimeters.
Then four.

Nico saw it and stiffened.
“Don’t let it reach the door,” he said.

“Why?” Touma asked.

“Because then the next room starts inheriting the problem.”

Touma stared.
That was the sort of answer that made the back of his neck go cold.

Mio’s expression changed.
Just a fraction.
She had heard something in that.

“What are you thinking?” Touma asked her.

She did not answer immediately.
That alone was enough to make him wary.

Then she said, “I’m thinking Kureha is no longer testing whether the collapse can be contained.”

Touma’s grip tightened.

“Then what is he testing?”

Mio looked at him.
Her eyes were very bright in the bad light.

“Whether coherence can survive once it has learned to walk.”

Touma did not like that sentence one bit.

Before he could ask what she meant, the wall speaker crackled.
Kureha’s voice came through again, calm and almost pleased.

“Subject display,” he said. “Excellent. The projection remains stable. Continue.”

Touma turned toward the wall. “You keep saying continue like you’re not trapping us in a room that’s forgetting itself.”

“Correct,” Kureha said. “I am not trapping you. I am observing the necessity of your mutual dependency under stress.”

Mio went still.
Touma felt it immediately.
Not just her fear.
Recognition.

He turned to her sharply. “What did he just say?”

Her lips parted.
Closed.
Opened again.

Then, very quietly, she said, “He’s not only using my clarity.”

Touma’s stomach sank.

“He’s using our pattern.”

Nico looked up from the console. “Meaning?”

Mio looked at Touma rather than Nico.
The look felt like a hand on the inside of his ribs.

“Meaning,” she said, “that the thing holding the room together is not just my stabilization signature. It’s the way Touma keeps choosing me anyway.”

Touma stopped breathing for half a second.

The statement was so naked that he almost rejected it on instinct.
But the room, cruelly, seemed to agree.
Every time he gripped her hand harder, the collapse stuttered.
Every time he tried to deny what that meant, the seams in the walls widened.

He stared at her.
Then, because the pressure in his chest had reached the point where only honesty could survive, he said, “You’re saying I’m part of the lock.”

Mio’s mouth twitched into something that might have been a grimace.
“Unfortunately.”

Touma let out a short, breathless laugh.
“That’s the nicest thing anyone has ever said to me.”

“It wasn’t nice.”

“It was honest.”

She looked away.
That was the first sign she was close to losing her own composure.
Touma’s pulse kicked harder.

Nico swore again. “If the room is indexing on your mutual coherence, then Kureha’s collapse model is basically feeding on the exact bond he’s trying to break.”

“Can you stop talking like a diagram?” Touma snapped.

“No,” Nico said. “I need you to understand this before you do something reckless.”

Touma looked at him. “I’m already in a room that’s forgetting itself.”

“That is precisely why.”

Mio’s hand tightened once.
A warning.
A request.
A plea, maybe, if she had ever been willing to use that shape.

Touma took a slow breath.
Then he looked at the far side of the room, where the seam in the floor had become a black line sharp enough to feel.

What remained?

The question echoed from Kureha’s voice.
Touma hated that it had a right to be asked.

What remained if a room forgot the people inside it?
What remained if his memory could no longer trust its own anchor?
What remained if Mio’s coherence was both shield and bait?

The answer rose in him before he could stop it.

Not the name.
Not yet.

Something adjacent.
Something uglier and more precious.
The refusal to let go.

He looked at Mio.
She looked back.
And in that look, Touma understood that the choice was not going to be between remembering and losing.
It was going to be between trying to preserve himself whole and using the part of himself that was already tied to her.

He hated that answer.
Which meant it was probably right.

Touma drew a careful breath.
“Mio,” he said.

“Don’t say it like that,” she replied immediately.

“Like what?”

“Like you’ve already decided.”

He gave a harsh laugh. “I have decided. I just don’t like the decision.”

That got him the smallest, sharpest flicker of her usual expression.
The one that meant she was both irritated and proud and trying not to show either.

Good.
He needed that.
He needed her like that, solid enough to be annoying.

Nico glanced between them. “Touma, if you’re thinking what I think you’re thinking, I want to say for the record that it is probably medically stupid.”

Touma didn’t look away from Mio.
“That’s your whole brand.”

Nico closed his eyes for a second.
“I hate that you’re not wrong.”

The wall speaker clicked.

Kureha’s voice returned, quieter now.
“Subject coherence rising in response to relational pressure. Very good.”

Touma’s jaw tightened so hard it hurt.

That was enough.
That was the thing.
Not the trap, not the machine, not the room.
The fact that Kureha was describing them as if affection were a fault line he could engineer around.

Touma looked at Mio’s hand still wrapped around his.
He could feel the cold of her skin.
He could feel the warmth arriving under it.
He could feel the difference between wanting to cling and choosing to stay.

And there, with the room shaking itself apart in tiny patient ways, he made the decision that mattered.

Not to trust the system.
Not to trust his memory.
To trust her.

“Okay,” he said.

Mio blinked. “That was too fast.”

“I’m trying not to make a speech.”

“Then don’t.”

He swallowed.
The missing edge inside him still hurt.
It still pulled.
But now it had somewhere to be.

He stepped closer to her.
Not enough to crowd her.
Enough to be clear.

“If the room is built around us staying coherent together,” Touma said, “then we use that.”

Mio’s eyes widened a fraction.
Nico’s head lifted.

Touma continued before he could second-guess himself.
“I’m not trying to remember the thing by force. I’m not doing this alone. If the missing name is tied to you and me both, then we stop treating that like a liability and start treating it like a target.”

Mio stared at him.
Her face had gone very still.

Then she said, carefully, “That sounds suspiciously like a plan.”

“It’s a bad one.”

“Obviously.”

He nodded once.
There was no room left for pretending this was anything else.

He felt the old instinct to recoil from using his power this way—the instinct that said every step deeper into certainty would cost him something he could not afford to lose.
But the room had already chosen the cost.
Now he chose the shape of it.

Touma looked at Nico. “Can you hold the room from closing in for ten seconds?”

Nico stared at him. “Probably not.”

“Try harder.”

“That is not how—”

“Ten seconds,” Touma said.

Nico exhaled sharply, then turned back to the console with the expression of a man who had decided there were worse ways to die than helping. “Fine. But if this makes things worse—”

“It will,” Mio said.

Touma snorted despite himself.
“Thanks for the confidence.”

Mio’s grip on his hand changed.
Not tighter.
Steadier.

A pulse went through the room.
The seams in the wall shivered.
Touma felt the missing shape in his memory call to him again, hard enough to make his eyes sting.

He did not reach for it directly.
Instead he reached for the line between knowing and not knowing.
The edge of certainty.
The place where Unthink could take hold.

Mio saw it instantly.
Her gaze sharpened.
“You want me to help you?”

Touma looked at her.
Then nodded once.

The answer was enough.

Mio inhaled.
The air around her brightened, a thin sheen of clarity gathering at the edges of her form.
Touma felt the room respond to her like a pupil narrowing.

Understood.

The thought was not his.
It was hers, but it landed in him like a brace.

Touma felt the first part of the power take hold.
Not on a thought in the room.
On the thought of the room.
On the shape of the collapse itself.

For one impossible second he saw Kureha’s model as a mesh of assumptions, labels, and carefully cultivated certainty.
He saw the procedure.
He saw the confidence it required.
He saw the room forgetting only because it had been taught how.

And then, with Mio holding the coherence line steady beside him, Touma struck the certainties behind it.
He was not rewriting the room.
He was negating the collapse’s claim to be the only stable reading while Mio held the remaining human pattern still enough to survive the hit.

Unthink.

The room jerked.
The speaker hissed.
Somewhere beyond the wall, Kureha made a sound of surprised irritation.

Touma’s own mind lurched in answer.

A memory tore loose.
Then another.

Not names.
Not yet.
But the emotional shape of what they guarded.
A hand in rain.
A laugh that mattered.
A promise made under cheap station lights.

Touma gasped.
Mio’s grip on his hand went painfully tight.

“Stay with me,” she said.

The words hit him like an order and a plea at once.

He did.

He pushed again.
Not harder.
Cleaner.
Through the fissure in the room’s certainty, through the model Kureha had built on recorded coherence, through the exact place where the missing name had been made absent.

For one terrible, brilliant instant, the whole collapse staggered.
The room lost its confident shape.
The seams in the floor snapped back together.
The speaker cut to static.

Touma felt Kureha’s certainty crack.
Not his body.
His plan.
His assumption that the room would obey the version of reality he had chosen for it.

Then the cost hit.

Not as pain.
As loss.

Touma’s vision flashed white, and for an instant he saw the keychain from the station in his palm.
He knew exactly who had given it to him.
He knew exactly why it mattered.
He knew the sound of the person’s voice in a way that made him want to turn and run toward them.

Then the memory collapsed out from under him.

He clutched at the feeling, but the feeling slid away with the name.

No.

Not the name.
A promise.

He lost the original reason he had chosen to keep trying to reach that person at all.

The emptiness was immediate and intimate and awful.
It did not feel like forgetting facts.
It felt like a piece of his chest had been quietly removed and replaced with a clean, unmarked absence.

Touma stumbled.

Mio caught him with both hands.
Her face had gone pale.
Not from fear for him alone.
From the fact that she felt the cut happen.

“Touma,” she said, and this time her voice broke a little, “what did you lose?”

He tried to answer.
Couldn’t.

The answer was already slipping behind the hole.

The room steadied with a violent shudder.
The door panel unlocked.
The wall speaker exploded into white noise.
Somewhere outside, footsteps ran.

And Kureha, no longer calm, said one word through the static:
“Impossible.”

Touma looked up.
The room was still there.
So were Nico and Mio.
So was the collapse, though weaker now, limping.

But the person-shaped reason behind the memory had gone.
Not their existence.
His emotional route to them.
The thing that made them matter to him in the old clean way was gone.

The cost should have ended the moment the room stopped shaking.
It didn’t.

Touma felt it still in the aftermath, a dull pressure under his ribs where the missing reason had been taken out cleanly enough to leave his body pretending the wound was only conceptual.

Mio held him upright by both shoulders. Her hands were steady, but the steadiness had a strain in it now, the kind that comes after someone keeps a door closed with their own body and then discovers the door was trying to make a room of them.

Nico was staring at the wall speaker as if glare alone might rebuild the sentence Kureha had lost.

The corridor beyond the room had gone uncomfortably quiet.
Not safe.
Just listening.

Touma swallowed and found his throat raw.
“What did I lose?” he asked again.

Mio’s expression did something he could not immediately read.
Not guilt.
Not exactly.
A decision deferred.

Before she could answer, the intercom hissed.
Kureha’s voice returned, thinner now, irritated in a way that sounded almost sincere.

“Containment is no longer sufficient,” he said.

Touma’s eyes narrowed. “You don’t get to sound surprised.”

“I’m not surprised,” Kureha replied. “I’m recalculating.”

“Same thing in your line of work.”

There was a pause.
Then, with the patience of a man explaining a procedure to an animal he had decided was smarter than expected, Kureha said, “The event has produced a bounded Unknow-adjacent state. Not perfect. But instructive.”

Touma felt his stomach twist.

Adjacent.
The word made the thing he had just done feel larger and worse.

Nico stared at him. “He’s calling it that on purpose.”

Touma looked at the speaker. “Yeah. I noticed.”

Mio’s grip tightened a fraction.

Touma looked at her.
She was pale enough now that the light seemed to pass through her a little more than before.
That should have scared him.
It did.
He just didn’t have the luxury of stopping.

“Kureha,” Mio said, and her voice was sharp enough to cut through the hum of the room, “if you keep trying to measure this state while the subject is still unstable, you’ll lose the ability to distinguish the holder from the held.”

Kureha answered at once. “That is precisely what I want to observe.”

Touma’s jaw tightened.
Of course it was.

The building shuddered again.
Not the collapse this time.
A response.
The whole facility now seemed to know that something had shifted and was trying to decide whether to call it damage or progress.

Touma looked around, forcing his vision to focus.
The room remained in one piece.
The chair was where it should have been. The cup was back on the sink. Nico’s tablet still displayed nonsense, but at least it was the same nonsense twice in a row.

The problem was not the room.
It was him.
The thing inside him that now had a missing contour.

He reached up before he could stop himself and pressed two fingers to his temple.
The pressure there felt wrong in a way he could not name.

Mio saw it instantly.
“Don’t,” she said.

“I wasn’t going to do anything.”

“You were checking the seam.”

Touma gave her a flat look. “You say that like it’s a bad habit.”

“It is.”

“It’s also useful.”

“In the same way a crowbar is useful.”

“That is unfair.”

“It is accurate.”

Nico let out a breath that might have been laughter if the air had been more optimistic.

The exchange might have been funny under different circumstances. Now it only made the ache under his ribs worse.

He still knew he had chosen.
He just no longer knew why that choice had once felt inevitable.

The knowledge kept trying to become shame.
He was too tired to let it.

He looked at Mio.
She was still watching him with that same careful attention, as if every blink from him might change what remained.

Touma exhaled slowly.
“Say it.”

Mio blinked. “Say what?”

“What you’re not saying.”

Nico muttered, “I hate this sentence.”

Mio ignored him.
Her gaze stayed on Touma.
For a second he thought she might refuse.
Then she said, “If I say the whole truth, you may try to compensate for the loss by forcing another retrieval.”

Touma nodded once. “And?”

“And if you do that,” she said, “you may lose the thread that lets you choose at all.”

The words landed cleanly.
Not because they were dramatic.
Because they fit the bruise in him too well.

Touma looked down at his own hand.
It was still trembling, though less now.
He curled it into a fist, then loosened it.

“Okay,” he said.

Mio went still.

Touma continued, because he was already past the point where backing up would make this less embarrassing.
“Then I don’t do that. I’m not going to turn myself into a hole because I hate not knowing.”

Nico blinked at him.
Mio’s expression shifted by the smallest degree.

“That is,” Nico said carefully, “the most sensible thing you’ve said all night.”

Touma shot him a tired glance. “Don’t make me regret it.”

“I wasn’t planning to.”

A faint movement crossed Mio’s mouth.
Almost a smile.
Not enough to call it one.
Enough to make Touma feel the absence in his chest answer to the sight of it.

Kureha let the silence sit long enough to become pressure again, then said, “Interesting.”

Touma looked up sharply. “You’re still here?”

“Adjacent,” Kureha corrected. “You made a partial break in the model. I am checking whether it can be reproduced.”

Touma barked a laugh. “You are unbelievably annoying.”

“Thank you.”

“That wasn’t a compliment.”

“It still counts if it’s accurate.”

Mio’s eyes narrowed at the wall. “You are mapping our responses.”

“Yes.”

“Using the collapse.”

“Yes.”

“With my clarity as the reference.”

“Yes.”

Touma looked at her. “You knew that already.”

Mio did not answer immediately.
Which was answer enough.

Touma’s jaw tightened.
The anger wanted somewhere to go.
If he let it, it would turn into the wrong kind of force.
He could feel that much already.

Instead he asked the question that hurt in a more useful direction.
“Mio. Did you let me do it?”

The room went quiet in a new way.
Nico stopped moving.
Even Kureha said nothing for a beat.

Mio’s gaze met Touma’s.
Then she looked away.
Not from guilt.
From the edge of the answer.

“I did not stop you,” she said.

Touma felt that land.
Not as betrayal.
As fact.

“Why?”

She inhaled.
The breath looked unneeded and therefore very deliberate.

“Because,” Mio said, “you needed to prove you could choose under loss, not just before it.”

Touma stared at her.

That was not an answer he liked.
That was not an answer that made him feel safe.
It was, infuriatingly, an answer that sounded like hers.

He searched her face for the joke hiding in it.
There wasn’t one.

“You decided that for me?” he asked quietly.

Mio’s jaw tightened.
“No.”

“No?”

“I decided what information to keep from you until you had to act.”

Touma’s stomach twisted.

“That is a very technical way to say yes.”

“It is a technical situation.”

He let out a breath.
A short laugh tried and failed to happen.

There it was.
The hard thing.
Not the power.
Not the collapse.
The fact that she had weighed his pain, the room, and the possibility of his becoming something worse, and chosen the version of him that could survive the hit.

He did not know whether to be grateful or furious.
Both, apparently.

Touma looked at her and said, “If you do that again, tell me you’re doing it.”

Mio blinked.
That was not what she had expected.
He could see that immediately.

“You want warning?”

“I want consent where it’s still possible.”

Nico made a very soft noise at that, the sound of a scientist realizing the patient is both smarter and more emotionally dangerous than anticipated.

Mio’s face changed again.
Subtle. Complicated. Less defensive.

“That’s not how the easiest version of this works,” she said.

“Good. I don’t want the easiest version.”

Her eyes widened by a fraction.

Touma felt the room hold its breath around them.

He continued, because if he stopped now he would chicken out and probably say something stupid instead.
“I want you to stay coherent. I want me to stay me. And I want us to stop treating it like there’s one clean answer in the wall.”

The silence after that was almost kind.

Then Kureha, from somewhere deeper in the facility, said, “That was revealing.”

Touma closed his eyes for a beat.
“Can you shut up for ten seconds?”

“No.”

“Rude.”

“Yes.”

Mio made a soft sound that might have been a laugh if it had more air in it.
That sound did something awful and beautiful to Touma’s chest.
Not repair.
Recognition.
He was still here.
She was still here.
The loss had not made the bond vanish.
It had changed the bond’s shape into something they had to hold differently.

That was the moment the corridor outside shifted again.
A new set of footsteps. Not staff. Heavier. Faster.
Someone slammed a hand against a door farther down the hall.
Then another.
The facility had decided the test was over in one room and was now trying the next.

Nico looked at the console and swore. “We need to move. Now.”

Touma nodded once.
He did not like moving blind.
He liked staying in place even less.

Mio’s hand found his again, not because she was afraid to lose him and not because he was afraid to lose the room.
Because both were true.
Because neither was enough on its own.

Touma looked at their joined hands and then at the dark hallway beyond the door.
He felt the missing reason like an ache in weather he could not see.
He hated it.
He also knew he was going to keep going.

That was the shape of the choice now.

“Okay,” he said again.

This time it sounded like a decision.

They moved.

The first corridor they entered was already in the middle of pretending not to be a corridor.

A staff member stood by a wall panel with a tablet in one hand and a look of very polite panic on his face. He looked up when they came through the door, recognized absolutely none of them, and still managed to sound professional.

“Please remain in designated support lanes,” he said.

Touma stopped dead. “You do not know who we are.”

The man frowned at his own sentence as if it had arrived from a file he had not opened. “I don’t think that matters.”

Mio’s voice went flat. “It matters a lot.”

The staffer glanced at her, frowned harder, then looked down at his tablet.

Whatever was on the screen clearly told him what to say next.

“Any person in this zone is required to undergo the standard self-verification sequence.”

Touma stared. “You are saying that like a normal thing.”

“It is a normal thing.”

Nico muttered, “It is absolutely not.”

The man blinked at him, then pressed the tablet closer to his chest like he had been threatened by tone.

Touma could hear people behind the walls now.
Quiet voices.
Repeated names.
Questions asked and re-asked in the same line of polite procedural terror.
The whole facility was being trained to confirm itself.

That should have been impossible.
It wasn’t.
It was happening.

He looked at Mio.
She was watching the staffer with a kind of distant fury he had only seen from her when she was trying not to panic.

“What’s the sequence?” Touma asked.

The staffer brightened, relieved to have a script.
“That depends on whether you are subject, support, or anomaly.”

Touma stared.
“That’s not a helpful category system.”

“It is a functioning one.”

Mio closed her eyes for a fraction of a second.
When she opened them, the irritation had sharpened into something more surgical.
“He’s not just filtering people,” she said to Touma. “He’s filtering permissions.”

Touma’s stomach sank.

Of course.

The room was not only forgetting.
It was assigning the right to remember.

The staffer held out the tablet.
“Please confirm your designation.”

Touma looked at the screen and saw three options already highlighted in pale blue:
SUBJECT, SUPPORT, CONTINGENCY.

None of them felt like a trap until he noticed the fourth line beneath them, smaller and harder to read:

UNVERIFIED WILL BE REROUTED.

Touma’s throat tightened.

Mio leaned in just enough to read it too.
Her expression went very still.

“Nico,” she said.

He read the screen and swore under his breath. “That’s not a safety protocol. That’s a sorting mechanism.”

The staffer looked offended.
“It is both.”

Touma looked at him.
Then at the corridor beyond, where more doors had gone open and shut in sequence like a building trying to remember how many mouths it had.

Every room had become a tiny tribunal.
Every person had become a category trying to survive the category that followed it.

He thought of the missing name and the way Kureha had set the collapse up around the exact place where people trusted labels to stay steady.

This wasn’t broad horror.
It was intimate.
Procedural.
Locally devastating.
He could hate it in detail.
That made it worse.

Touma looked back at the staffer. “What happens if we don’t choose?”

The man glanced at the tablet, then said with forced brightness, “Then the system chooses for you.”

Mio’s eyes narrowed.
“That,” she said, “is the most honest thing anyone here has said.”

The staffer looked even more alarmed, as if honesty had not been one of the acceptable outputs.

Then the corridor lights shifted.
A soft blue line ran down the wall and stopped beside a door marked 3B.
A voice over the facility speakers announced, “Verification route active.”

Touma turned slowly.
The route markers were leading them onward again.
Not away from the problem.
Through the problem.

He could hear a woman crying behind one door.
Then a man’s voice trying to coach her through her own name.
Then the pause when the name failed to land and everyone in the room had to pretend they had not watched a human being lose footing under polite fluorescent lights.

Touma felt sick.

This was what Kureha meant by local.
Not scales.
People.
Doors.
Steps.
A hallway where every interaction had become a test of whether you still counted as yourself after being corrected.

Mio touched his sleeve.
Not a pull.
An anchor.

“Don’t look at every room,” she murmured. “We need the route.”

Touma swallowed.
“I hate that you’re right.”

“You usually do.”

He managed a short, broken exhale that almost became a laugh.
Then the cry from the room behind the door cut off too suddenly.
The silence that followed was worse.

Touma turned toward the sound before he could stop himself.
The door had a little window.
Through it he saw three staff members standing around a seated woman with a clipboard and a witness monitor.
One of them was speaking in a tone so calm it made Touma’s skin prickle.

“Please restate your role in the event from the point where your certainty degraded.”

The woman looked up, dazed, and whispered, “I was the one who remembered.”

One of the staff members wrote it down.

Touma felt something in him go cold and hard.

That was the procedure.
Not just losing names.
Being forced to narrate the moment you lost them while the room watched for permission to let you keep speaking.

He took one step forward.
Mio caught his arm.

“Touma.”

He looked at her.

She was not warning him because of fear.
She was warning him because she knew the look on his face.
Because he was about to become useful in exactly the wrong way.

He exhaled once.
Then forced himself back.

“Yeah,” he said. “I know.”

The staffer with the tablet suddenly looked at them harder.
It was the first real recognition he’d shown, though it did not seem to know what to do with the recognition once it arrived.

“You’re not matching the route profile,” he said.

Touma stared. “I’m sorry, the what?”

“The route profile,” the man repeated, then winced as if reading the phrase gave him a headache. “If you do not correspond to any verified recovery pathway, you will need to be escorted.”

Nico laughed once, humorless. “Escorted where?”

The man looked genuinely afraid now.

“Deeper,” he said.

Touma’s stomach dropped.

Of course that was the answer.

The people running the collapse did not want anyone to leave it accidentally.
They wanted the uncertain things moved inward, where certainty could be audited.

Mio’s hand slid down Touma’s arm until their fingers interlaced.
It was such a small motion.
It made the hallway seem less able to swallow him whole.

Touma looked at her.

“You knew this was coming,” he said quietly.

Mio did not pretend otherwise.

“Yes.”

“Did you know how bad?”

“No.”

That was enough honesty to matter.

He nodded.
The thing in his chest that was still trying to be angry settled into something more usable.

Mio saw the change before he had words for it.

“What are you thinking?” she asked.

Touma looked past her at the route markers glowing down the hall like a polite invitation to become evidence.

“That ‘deeper’ only works if we agree he gets to define what we are.”

Nico’s head snapped up.

Mio’s eyes sharpened. “Yes.”

The staffer with the tablet frowned at them as if he could feel the sentence refusing to stay procedural.
“Please remain on the marked path.”

Touma looked at him. “What path?”

The man blinked.
Then looked down at the tablet, where the answer was clearly supposed to be.

“The verified recovery pathway,” he said, but the phrase came out weaker this time.

Mio stepped half a pace closer to Touma without letting go of his hand.
“Recovery from what?”

The staffer opened his mouth.
Nothing immediate arrived.

That was the fracture.
Small.
Real.

Touma felt it travel through the corridor like a hairline crack through glass.
The route markers did not go dark.
They hesitated.
One arrow brightened, then dimmed, then pointed very slightly the wrong way.

Nico stared at the wall panel by the door. “Oh,” he said, with the horrified fascination of a man watching a theory become a felony. “The route profile is not just tracking identity. It’s being generated by asserted certainty.”

Kureha’s voice entered from the speaker overhead at once.
“Do not improvise.”

Touma looked up.
The calm in that voice was intact.
Too intact.
Which meant Kureha had heard the crack too.

“Why?” Touma asked. “Afraid the hallway might start thinking for itself?”

“Afraid,” Kureha said, “that you will confuse destabilization with freedom.”

Mio’s laugh was short and sharp enough to count as a weapon.
“You built a prison out of category pressure,” she said. “Don’t flatter yourself by calling the door safety.”

For one beat, even the staffer looked like he wanted that to be true.

Kureha answered her, still level. “If the system cannot determine what you are, it must move you inward until determination becomes possible.”

Touma’s grip tightened around Mio’s hand.
There it was.
Not just the cruelty.
The shape of it.
Kureha did not need them broken in some dramatic cosmic sense.
He needed them made legible on his terms.
Measured. Routed. Named.

Which meant the answer was not bigger force.
It was refusal.

He looked at Mio.
She was already there.
Of course she was.

“Don’t chase the missing piece,” she said quietly.
“Use the gap.”

Touma swallowed.
That should have sounded impossible.
Instead it sounded like standing on the exact edge of something he had already survived once tonight.

Nico took one step back toward the workspace door and slapped his palm against the manual release pad.
“If you’re doing this,” he said, “do it fast. The system is trying to spawn a deeper-route correction.”

The corridor lights shifted.
New arrows began to bloom under the old ones.
Doors further down the hall clicked in sequence.
One.
Two.
Three.
Like the building had started unlatching itself from the inside.

Touma looked at the staffer again.
The man’s badge was turned sideways.
His tablet kept trying to populate a field that would not stay filled.
Subject.
Witness.
Escort.
Revision risk.
Every label flickered, landed, then slid.

Touma heard himself say, very clearly, “No.”

It was not loud.
It did not need to be.

He was not saying no to the staffer.
Or even to Kureha.
He was saying no to the whole demand that this moment become legible only by being reduced.

The missing name inside him flared like pain.
The old instinct rose at once: catch it, force it, make the wound produce a usable answer.

He did not.

He let the absence stay absent.
He let it remain a real thing without letting the room turn it into a category.
A person-shaped hole.
A reason-shaped wound.
A choice still standing after its explanation had been damaged.

Beside him, Mio inhaled sharply.
Then steadied.

“Yes,” she said.
Not agreement with the room.
Agreement with him.
With the refusal.

The effect was immediate.

The route lights along the floor strobed once and split.
Half the arrows pointed inward.
Half pointed nowhere at all.
The door beside the staffer flashed RECOVERY, then OBSERVATION, then a string of characters Touma could not read because the hallway itself seemed to lose confidence halfway through naming them.

Somewhere deeper in the facility, an alarm changed tone.
Not escalation.
Confusion.

Kureha’s voice cut back in, and for the first time all day it carried strain.
“Stabilize the subject path.”

“No,” Touma said again, and this time the word came with teeth.
He took one step forward, dragging the impossible certainty of his damaged choice with him. “You don’t get to decide what survives by deciding what counts.”

The staffer recoiled.
Not because Touma had threatened him.
Because the tablet in his hands had just gone blank.

Nico made a startled sound.
“The facility loop is eating its own verification stack,” he said. “He broke the precedence chain.”

Mio’s fingers tightened around Touma’s hard enough to hurt.
He understood the warning inside it at once.
Too far and he would tear himself open trying to hold the gap.
Too little and Kureha would reseal the route around them.

So he did the hardest thing.
He stayed exactly there.

Not certain.
Not collapsed.
Not named cleanly enough to be rerouted.

Mio moved with him, shoulder brushing his.
Her voice, when she spoke, was low and steady.
“We are here,” she said into the corridor.
“We are not your corrected version.”

The hallway answered by forgetting what shape it had been trying to keep.
The second set of arrows vanished.
The first set scattered into harmless guide-lights.
Three doors further down the hall unlocked at once, then all their status panels went dark.
The speaker above them hissed with blown static.

And through it, like a man trying not to sound wounded by his own surprise, Kureha said, “Interesting.”

Touma laughed once.
It came out ragged.
“God, I hate you.”

“That,” Mio said, still holding on, “also seems structurally sound.”

Nico pointed hard toward the workspace. “Move. Now. Before the system picks a new theory.”

They moved.
Still hand in hand.
Still carrying the live fracture with them.

Behind them, the corridor lights flashed one last time.
Touma heard doors unlatch across the facility, heard voices rise, heard the exact second the local procedure stopped behaving like an omniscient process and started sounding like frightened humans with clipboards.

By the time Nico got the workspace door sealed behind them and threw himself at the console, the pressure in the air had changed.
Less hungry.
Less sure.
Still dangerous, but no longer cleanly aligned.

“The collapse is no longer propagating on the same chain,” Nico said, hands flying over the controls. “Hold exactly whatever the hell that was.”

Touma barely heard him.
The room could stop shaking.
Kureha’s route could break.
The model could lose its clean authority.
None of that restored the thing that had been taken.

Mio did not let go of him.
He looked down at their joined hands and felt the answer arrive in the worst possible shape: the choice was still here.
The old reason was not.

That was the victory.
That was the wound.

Somewhere out in the hallway, an alarm kept clicking in a bad rhythm.
The room stopped trying to erase itself.
Touma still could not remember why the missing person had once mattered enough to tear him open.
He only knew that when he looked at Mio, the damaged choice remained.
And that hurt more than panic.

### 12 — What Remains, What Stays

The first thing Touma learned after the room stopped trying to erase itself was that silence could still feel aggressive.

Not dead silence.
Worse.
The kind that followed an emergency when everyone was pretending they had not just nearly lost something they could not name.

A broken alarm somewhere in the hallway kept clicking in a bad rhythm.
The lights had come back half-strength, enough to reveal the damage and not enough to be forgiving about it.
Nico stood by the console with one hand braced on the edge, looking like a man who had survived a technical catastrophe only to discover it now counted as his personality.

Mio was still holding Touma’s hand.

He had not let go.
She had not either.
That should have felt embarrassing by now.
Instead it felt necessary in a way he did not have the language to defend.

Touma swallowed and tried to sort out the shape of himself.
Some things were gone.
He knew that immediately.
Not from an intellectual accounting.
From the clean, sick feeling of reaching for a familiar shelf inside his head and finding that someone had removed one whole section without leaving a note.

A person mattered.
A promise mattered.
A reason mattered.

The edges of all three were there.
The center was not.

Mio watched his face without speaking.
That was her way of being gentle, which was still so rude it almost made him laugh.

Nico cleared his throat. “The collapse is no longer actively propagating.”

Touma looked at him. “That was your nicest sentence all day.”

“Lower your standards.”

“I’m trying.”

Mio said, “He’s doing his best not to sound relieved.”

Nico’s expression tightened. “I am relieved.”

Touma gave him a tired look. “That sounds like a lie you should feel worse about.”

“It isn’t a lie. It’s a professional habit.”

Touma might have answered, but the corridor outside the workspace shifted with movement. Voices. Footsteps. Too many people deciding at once that the crisis had become their responsibility now that it was no longer contained.

Mio’s grip on his hand changed again.
Not fear.
Alertness.

Touma felt it too: the room had stopped forgetting, but the facility had not returned to normal. The damage had only narrowed into a shape people could walk toward.

Nico glanced at the door. “We don’t have much time before someone with authority comes looking for the official version.”

Touma’s head hurt enough that the phrase almost made him angry on reflex.
“What happened to the unofficial version?”

Nico looked at Mio, then at Touma.
“It’s standing right there.”

Mio raised an eyebrow. “I dislike being summarized.”

“That makes two of us,” Touma muttered.

He shifted his balance carefully. The room did not lurch, which was already an improvement over the last hour of his life.
He looked down at his own hand, still wrapped around Mio’s.
There was a bruise forming around his wrist where she had caught him during the collapse.
The sight felt absurdly intimate.
He wondered whether bruises counted as memory.
Probably not.
He wished they did.

“Mio,” he said.

She looked up.

He almost asked the obvious question.
What did I lose?
Who was it?
Can you tell me?

But the panic from before was still too close, and he could tell by the way her shoulders held that she had no desire to yank the missing thing back into daylight unless he was ready to break around it.

So he asked the only thing that mattered.
“Am I still me?”

For the first time since the collapse, her expression softened without becoming a joke.

“Yes,” she said immediately.
“Damaged,” she added, because she was still herself and could not help it.
“But yes.”

Touma let out a breath he had not realized he was holding.

Nico looked between them. “That’s not the same as full continuity.”

“I know,” Touma said.

Mio’s fingers squeezed once around his hand.
Not enough to hurt.
Enough to make a point.

Touma looked at her.
The impulse to apologize for whatever he had lost rose and then failed halfway.
He did not owe her a clean story.
He owed her honesty.

“I remember the shape of choosing,” he said slowly.

Mio went still.

Touma kept going before he lost the nerve.
“I don’t remember the whole reason. Not all of it. But I remember that when it mattered, I chose to keep reaching for you anyway.”

Mio’s face changed in the smallest possible way.
The effect hit him anyway.

That was the thing about her.
She did not need dramatic reactions to matter.
She mattered by surviving the exact size of honesty he could manage.

Nico looked uncomfortable in the way of someone who had accidentally walked into a moment and understood enough to know he should not comment.
So naturally he did.

“For the record,” he said, “that choice is the important part.”

Touma looked at him with flat exhaustion. “That’s your contribution?”

“It’s a decent contribution.”

“It’s a terrible contribution.”

“It’s one I stand by.”

Mio made a sound that might have been amusement if she had not still been watching Touma like he could fracture if she blinked wrong.

Outside the room, the footsteps got closer, then stopped.
A voice Touma did not recognize said something about containment protocols.
Another voice answered with the brittle confidence of an admin trying to pretend they had planned for all of this.

Touma’s body tensed.
The old urge to protect the remaining scraps of the moment hit him hard.
He did not want the room to become public property yet.
He did not want anyone else naming what had happened before he could understand it himself.

Mio noticed.
Of course she did.

“Touma,” she said quietly, “breathe.”

He shot her a look. “You don’t get to sound like my doctor.”

“I very much do.”

“No, you don’t.”

“I absolutely do.”

Nico muttered, “This is somehow helping.”

Touma glared at him. “Whose side are you on?”

“The side where the room remains approximately intact.”

Touma almost smiled.
Almost.

Then Mio’s thumb brushed over his knuckles, and the almost became something real enough to hurt.

He realized, with a tired little jolt, that the choice he had made in the collapse was not a one-time heroic act.
It was a new fact.
One he would have to keep making.

Choose her.
Not because he remembered the original clean reason.
Because he knew what the act cost, and he still chose it.

That was more honest than memory ever was.

The door panel shuddered.
Someone outside said, “Open it.”

Nico looked at Touma. “I can delay them thirty seconds if I have to.”

Touma exhaled.
“Why would you have to?”

Nico’s face settled into an expression that was almost grim.
“Because the next official question is going to be whether your interface survived the event.”

Touma glanced at Mio.
Her jaw tightened.

That made him look back at Nico.
“What did you call her?”

Nico paused.
Then, carefully: “Interface.”

Touma frowned. “That’s not what she is.”

“No,” Mio said, and the word was sharp enough to cut.

The corridor quieted for a beat outside.
Then, from the hallway speaker, a new voice entered the facility with clean administrative calm.

“Kureha Sanjo, oversight review,” it said. “I need confirmation that the subject’s limiter projection has not degraded into a revision-class anomaly.”

Touma stared at the speaker.

Even after everything, Kureha’s voice was still infuriatingly composed.
That made Touma want to break something.

Mio’s eyes narrowed.
She looked not at the speaker, but at the space around it, as if she could sense the shape of the next trap before it fully entered the room.

Touma felt the air change.
He did not understand the mechanism.
He understood the pressure.

Kureha continued, almost pleasantly. “If the subject remains coherent, I will need to speak with both of you.”

Nico let out one short, disbelieving breath.
“That,” he said, “is not a victory statement. That is a man asking for confirmation because his model did not survive contact with reality.”

Touma felt the truth of that land at once.
Kureha was still dangerous.
Still in the building.
Still trying to name what they had become.
But the clean version of the event—the one where the room proved personhood could be corrected into something manageable—was gone.
They had broken that.

Mio’s hand tightened on Touma’s.
Not warning.
Decision.

Touma looked at her and found, under the fear and the fatigue, a steadier thing.
She was still here.
Not as a voice in his skull.
Not as a problem to be fixed.
A person standing beside him in a room that had tried very hard to make that impossible.

He took a breath.
The missing piece in his head still ached.
The original reason was gone.
But the choice did not care.

Touma straightened a little and said toward the speaker, “You can talk to us through the door.”

There was a pause.
Then Kureha, with clear amusement, answered, “No, Touma. I think I’d prefer to see what remains when the naming pressure starts again.”

The lights flickered once.
Mio’s eyes lifted to the ceiling.

Then, very quietly, she said, “Touma.”

He looked at her.

If she had wanted to joke, she didn’t.
If she had wanted to hide, she didn’t.
She just held his gaze with the kind of seriousness that made him believe in her more than any proof could.

The silence after Kureha’s line felt less like a pause and more like a hand held over a switch.

Touma looked at Mio.
She looked back.
Neither of them moved.
That, in its own way, was movement too.
Not retreat.
Not advance.
A decision to exist in the same second without letting Kureha force the shape of it.

Nico swore softly beside the console. “He’s not leaving.”

Touma glanced at the speaker. “That’s obvious.”

“No,” Nico said, eyes narrowed at the diagnostic feed, “I mean he’s physically close enough to keep broadcasting from inside the facility loop.”

Touma’s stomach turned.
So Kureha hadn’t just attacked them remotely.
He had set himself up somewhere in the building like a patient at the center of his own procedure.

Of course he had.

Mio’s grip on Touma’s hand changed again, and he realized with a small jolt that she was reading the room before he was.
Not the collapse.
The aftermath.
The people who would come running now that the worst had already been named.

He looked at her.
“You okay?”

The question sounded inadequate the second he said it.
Mio still answered it anyway.
“No.”

That was so immediate it almost startled a laugh out of him.
She saw that and rolled her eyes a little, which somehow made her more convincing.

“Not okay,” she repeated. “But functional.”

Touma nodded.
That felt honest in a way no reassurance would have.

A shape moved in the hallway.
A guard? A nurse? Someone from operations with a clipboard and a terrible sense of timing.
Touma braced himself for another question.
Instead, the person outside the door spoke to someone else with the shaky professionalism of a human trying to keep a catastrophe in a category.

“Do we have the subject identity log back yet?”

A second voice answered, “Not cleanly.”

Touma felt a tiny, angry jolt.
Not cleanly.
That phrasing made memory sound like a spill.

Mio felt him tense and squeezed his hand once.
He looked down.
Her fingers were still wrapped around his, but now there was a tired softness to the way she held on. Less emergency. More insistence.
That was new.
Or maybe it had always been there and he just had not had room for it before.

The doorway remained shut.
No one came in.
The sounds outside shifted from crisis into cleanup.
The tones got lower. Slower. More procedural.
Touma hated that almost as much as the fear.

Nico finally sank into the chair beside the console and rubbed both hands over his face.
“I need about six months and three ethical violations to parse this properly.”

Touma snorted despite himself.
“That’s your normal rate.”

“Not on a day like this.”

Mio glanced at Nico.
Then at Touma.
Then, in a voice too quiet to be casual, she said, “He’s trying to make the aftermath feel like consent.”

Touma looked at her.
“Who?”

“Kureha.”

He nodded once.
Yeah, that made sense in the ugliest possible way.
The room had been forced into coherence by pain, and now the paperwork would arrive to call the shape of that pain an accepted condition.

Something metal rattled past the hallway outside.
A cart, maybe. Instruments. Emergency storage bins. The sound snagged on the warped acoustics of the facility and came back in little broken pieces, as if even the cleanup could not agree on what had happened yet.

A woman somewhere beyond the door said, with exhausted authority, “Tag every room that had direct route exposure.”
Another voice answered, “We’re trying. The door numbers won’t stay put.”

Touma closed his eyes for one second.
That landed harder than he wanted it to.
Not because it was new.
Because it meant the damage had made it out of the moment and into procedure.
Now there would be forms for it.
Review language.
Sanctioned phrases for how to speak about people after a system had failed to keep them continuous.

When he opened his eyes again, the glass panel in the door had gone faintly reflective.
For a breath, he saw himself and Mio there together, thinned by the bad lighting but recognizably side by side.
No lag this time.
No double image.
Just both of them looking more tired than the room deserved.

That should not have felt like mercy.
It did.

Mio followed his gaze to the reflection and then away again, too fast to be casual.
He felt the movement in her hand before he looked down.
Her grip had changed once more.
Still firm.
Less tactical now.
Almost protective.
Maybe of him.
Maybe of the fact that they were still, against all evidence, both standing here.

Touma’s jaw tightened.
“I hate this guy.”

Mio answered instantly, “Good. Keep that.”

That should have been a joke.
It wasn’t.
It was advice.

Touma let out a slow breath.
The ache under his ribs had settled into a bandaged absence.
Permanent, maybe.
But not the only thing in the room anymore.

He looked at Mio again.
What had changed was not her.
It was his ability to pretend she had ever been just utility.

She had kept him coherent and paid for it.
That mattered.

Touma cleared his throat.
“I need to say something.”

Mio’s expression sharpened at once. “That line never means anything good.”

“Rude.”

“Accurate.”

He huffed a laugh and then stopped because the laugh threatened to turn into grief.
He pushed through it.

“I know I don’t remember the whole reason,” he said. “But I remember the shape of choosing. And I don’t want to act like that was an accident just because I can’t reconstruct it.”

Mio went very still.
Nico, who had clearly learned enough by now to know when to become furniture, looked away from them with deliberate care.

Touma continued, slower now.
“If I chose you once with the original reason and choose you again now without it, that still counts.”

Mio’s mouth parted slightly.
For the first time all night, she looked caught off guard.

Touma swallowed.
He could hear his own pulse.
He forced himself to keep going.
“I’m not saying I’m fine. I’m not saying it doesn’t hurt. I’m saying I’m not going to treat the missing piece like proof that the rest was fake.”

Mio stared at him.
For a second he thought she might make a joke, maybe because she needed one.
Instead her eyes turned a little glassy in the way she probably would have denied if asked.

“That,” she said carefully, “is much more active than I expected.”

Touma felt heat rise in his face.
“Was that not the correct thing to say?”

“It was,” Mio said.
Then, after a beat: “It was just more honest than your usual performances.”

He gave her a flat look.
“That’s a low bar.”

“It is.”

She squeezed his hand.
This time the pressure wasn’t only grounding.
It was answering.

Touma looked at the bruise on his wrist again.
Then back at her.
“I don’t want to be carried through this forever,” he said.

Mio’s eyes narrowed. “I was not carrying you.”

“You absolutely were.”

“I was managing a catastrophic coherence event.”

“That sounds like carrying with extra steps.”

Nico made a choked sound that might have been amusement.
Mio shot him a look, then back at Touma.
The irritation was there, but so was something warmer.
Something that made his chest ache in a different register.

“You were barely upright,” she said.

“I was upright enough.”

“You were emotionally vertical at best.”

Touma stared. “That is an insane sentence.”

“And yet.”

He looked at her face, at the exact shape of the projection he now knew could be hurt in more ways than one.
The knowledge made him careful in a way that felt new.
Not afraid to touch.
Aware that touch meant something now.

He let the silence sit between them for a second.
Then he said, “I want to keep doing this with you.”

Mio’s expression changed so fast it almost passed him by.
Not surprise.
Not exactly.
The first real arrival of hope, maybe, and all the caution that came with it.

“You say that like it’s a request,” she said.

“It is.”

“That’s annoying.”

“Yeah.”

“Because if it’s a request, I have to answer it.”

Touma’s throat tightened.
He nodded once.
“Yeah.”

The room stayed quiet around them.
No collapse.
No alarm.
Just the wet, ugly residue of what had happened and the fact that neither of them had been washed away by it.

Mio looked down at their joined hands.
Then back up at him.

“Then my answer is,” she said, “you should probably keep me nearby if you expect to remain alive.”

Touma blinked.
That was so her he almost laughed.

Then she added, quieter, “And yes.”

The word landed in him harder than anything Kureha had said.
Because it was not a diagnosis.
Not a function.
Not a report.
It was a choice.

A new one.

Touma’s hand closed gently around hers.
He realized, as he did it, that this was the first time since the collapse that he had chosen the contact without needing it to stabilize him.
He wanted the contact because she had answered.
Because she was still here.
Because he wanted to answer back.

That distinction mattered more than he could have explained.

The corridor outside grew busy again.
People moving.
A stretcher wheel hitting a seam in the floor.
Someone giving instructions in a voice that tried too hard to sound normal.
The facility was resuming its shape around the damage.

Touma looked toward the door.
Then back at Mio.

“There’s going to be fallout,” he said.

“Obviously.”

“Are you okay with that?”

Mio gave him a look so dry it almost counted as affection.
“No. But I am here.”

Touma nodded.
That was enough.
Maybe not forever.
Enough for now.

The wall speaker flickered once.
For a moment Touma thought Kureha was about to speak again.
Instead the screen over the console lit with a single recovered diagnostic line.

UNREVISION POSSIBLE: NO.

Below it, in a smaller warning string they had definitely not seen before, the display added:

NEXT STATE: UNREVISE CANDIDATE.

Mio went very still.
Touma stared.

Nico read the line and made a sound like he wished he had not.

Touma looked at Mio.
She looked at the screen, then at him.
The fear there was controlled, but not hidden.

Touma felt the future shift by half a degree.
Not enough to become an explanation.
Enough to become a threat.

He opened his mouth.
Closed it.
Then, because the room had already taken enough from them for one night, he chose the only honest thing left.
He stepped closer to Mio, very deliberately, and said, “We’ll deal with that later.”

Mio’s expression softened into something tired and fond and deeply unimpressed.

“Bold of you to assume later is generous.”

Touma huffed a laugh.
It hurt a little.
It was worth it.

Outside, someone knocked once on the door and called his name.
Touma did not move right away.
He looked at Mio one more time.
Then at the recovered line on the monitor.
Then back to her.

This was the part where he would have, before, reached for the old reason.
Now he reached for the new one.
Not repair.
Not certainty.
The active decision to stay.

He squeezed her hand.
Mio squeezed back.

Then he turned toward the door.
